# Chapter 8: Alternative Pooled Investments

> REITs, hedge funds and direct participation programs (DPPs). Shorter chapter, but each product has a handful of reliably tested facts.

---

## 8.1 Real estate investment trusts (REITs)

A **REIT** is a company that owns, operates or finances income-producing real estate and sells shares to investors, letting them participate in real estate without buying property directly.

**Three types.**

| Type | Invests in | Income source |
|---|---|---|
| **Equity REIT** | Owns and operates properties (apartments, office buildings, malls, hotels, warehouses, data centers) | Rents and property appreciation |
| **Mortgage REIT** | Lends to property owners or buys mortgages/MBS | Interest; sensitive to interest rates |
| **Hybrid REIT** | Both | Both |

**Tax treatment — the key testable rule.** A REIT avoids corporate income tax if it:

- Distributes at least **90%** of its taxable income to shareholders as dividends (a conduit, like Subchapter M), and
- Has at least **75%** of its assets in real estate (and derives ≥ 75% of gross income from real estate sources).

REIT dividends are generally taxed to investors as **ordinary income** (not qualified dividends), though investors may be eligible for a 20% qualified business income deduction. **Losses are not passed through** to shareholders — this is a major difference from DPPs/limited partnerships. Only income flows through.

**Trading and structure.**

- Most REITs are **publicly traded on exchanges (NYSE)** — liquid, priced by the market, bought with commissions like any stock.
- **Non-traded (public, non-listed) REITs** are registered with the SEC but don't trade; they are illiquid (redemption programs are limited), carry high upfront fees (often 10%+), and have raised FINRA suitability concerns. Values on statements may not reflect what an investor could actually get.
- **Private REITs** are unregistered and sold to accredited investors.
- REITs are **not** investment companies under the 1940 Act, even though they look like closed-end funds. They are registered under the 1933 Act and trade under the 1934 Act.
- Not redeemable (negotiable).

**Why investors buy REITs.** Regular, relatively high **dividend income**; diversification into real estate; liquidity (for listed REITs); an inflation hedge (rents and property values tend to rise with inflation). REITs are also a modest hedge against the stock market because real estate has lower correlation with equities.

**Risks.** Real estate market risk, interest-rate risk (rising rates hurt REIT prices, especially mortgage REITs), leverage, and — for non-traded REITs — severe liquidity risk and high fees.

## 8.2 Hedge funds

A **hedge fund** is a privately organized investment pool, usually structured as a **limited partnership**, that uses a wide range of strategies — leverage, short selling, derivatives, arbitrage, distressed debt, currencies, commodities — to seek positive ("absolute") returns in any market.

**Regulation and access.**

- Sold as **private placements** under **Regulation D**, so they are **not registered** with the SEC under the 1933 Act, and most are **exempt from the Investment Company Act of 1940** (3(c)(1) funds with ≤ 100 investors or 3(c)(7) funds limited to "qualified purchasers").
- Available only to **accredited investors** (net worth over $1 million excluding primary residence, or income over $200,000 individually / $300,000 jointly in each of the last two years) and institutions. Many require much higher minimums ($1 million+) and limit themselves to qualified purchasers ($5 million+ in investments).
- Minimal disclosure requirements — no prospectus; an offering memorandum instead. Large advisers must register with the SEC as investment advisers.
- Hedge fund **managers** often register under the Investment Advisers Act of 1940.

**Characteristics the exam tests.**

- **Illiquid** — lock-up periods (often 1 year+) and redemption only at quarterly or annual windows with notice.
- **High fees** — the classic "**2 and 20**": a 2% annual management fee plus 20% of profits (performance/incentive fee). Many have a **high-water mark** (performance fee only on new gains).
- **Leverage and derivatives** → potentially large, fast losses; the fund itself can be wiped out (limited partners cannot lose more than they invested).
- **Lack of transparency** — strategies and holdings are proprietary.
- **Less regulatory oversight** than mutual funds.
- Returns are often **uncorrelated** with the broad market, which is the diversification argument.

**Fund of hedge funds** — a fund that invests in several hedge funds. Can be registered under the 1940 Act and sold to retail investors with lower minimums, but adds a **second layer of fees** and still carries the underlying funds' illiquidity and risk.

**Suitability.** Only for sophisticated, wealthy investors with high risk tolerance who can afford to lock up money and potentially lose it. Not for retail investors seeking liquidity or transparency.

## 8.3 Direct participation programs

A **direct participation program (DPP)** is a business — almost always a **limited partnership (LP)** (sometimes an LLC or S corporation) — that passes income, gains, **losses**, deductions and tax credits directly through to investors, avoiding corporate taxation. "Direct participation" means investors participate directly in the cash flow and tax consequences.

**Structure.**

| Party | Role | Liability |
|---|---|---|
| **General partner (GP)** | Manages the business, makes all decisions, has a fiduciary duty to the LPs, must keep at least a 1% interest, can't compete with the partnership | **Unlimited** personal liability |
| **Limited partners (LPs)** | Passive investors; provide capital; vote on major matters (sale of assets, change of GP, dissolution); may sue the GP; may inspect books | **Limited** to their investment (plus any recourse debt they agreed to) — but they must **not** participate in management or they risk losing limited status |

Documents: **certificate of limited partnership** (filed with the state), **partnership agreement**, and a **subscription agreement** that the investor signs and the GP must **accept** — the GP decides whether to admit an investor. Investors are usually required to disclose net worth and income because DPPs have suitability thresholds.

**Types of programs.**

- **Real estate** — raw land (speculative, appreciation only, no depreciation), new construction, existing properties (income + depreciation), government-assisted housing (tax credits).
- **Oil and gas** — **exploratory ("wildcat")** programs (highest risk/reward), **developmental** (drilling near proven reserves), **income** programs (buy producing wells — lowest risk, immediate income), and combination programs. Tax benefits: **intangible drilling costs (IDCs)** are deductible immediately; **depletion** allowances for the resource used up.
- **Equipment leasing** — buy equipment (aircraft, railcars, computers) and lease it; income plus depreciation deductions.
- **Other:** cattle feeding, cable TV, agriculture.

**Tax features.**

- Income and **losses** flow through to partners' personal returns (unlike REITs and corporations). Historically, large early-year losses from depreciation were the main attraction ("tax shelters").
- Since the 1986 tax reform, DPP losses are **passive losses** that can offset only **passive income** (from other DPPs or rental activities), not wages or portfolio income. Unused passive losses carry forward.
- **Depreciation** (real estate, equipment) and **depletion** (oil, gas, minerals) are the main non-cash deductions.
- **Recapture** — when the asset is sold, previously deducted depreciation may be taxed as ordinary income.
- An investor's **cost basis** = cash invested + share of recourse debt (and, for real estate, non-recourse debt). Losses can't exceed basis.
- At dissolution, assets are distributed in order: secured creditors → general creditors → limited partners → general partner.

**Risks and drawbacks.**

- **Illiquidity** — no secondary market to speak of; transferring an interest usually requires GP approval. This is the number-one risk tested.
- Long holding periods (often 7–15 years).
- Business risk of the venture; GP competence.
- Tax-law risk (benefits can be reduced by legislation); the IRS may challenge "abusive" shelters.
- Complex **K-1** tax reporting for investors.
- High upfront costs and fees.

**Evaluating a DPP.** Economic viability of the venture is the most important factor — a program should make sense as a business apart from tax benefits. Also consider the GP's track record, the program's objectives and timeline, start-up costs, and the ability to borrow/leverage.

**Suitability.** High net worth, high tax bracket investors who need passive income to shelter or want the specific asset exposure, have a long time horizon and can tolerate illiquidity. Most states and sponsors impose minimum income/net-worth standards. Not for investors who need liquidity, and the tax benefits are wasted in retirement accounts.

**Comparison table — the exam's favorite.**

| Feature | REIT | Hedge fund | DPP (LP) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Liquidity | Listed: high; non-traded: low | Low (lock-ups) | Very low |
| Passes through income | Yes (90% rule) | Yes (partnership) | Yes |
| Passes through **losses** | **No** | Yes (partnership) | **Yes** |
| Investor type | Anyone (listed) | Accredited only | Usually net-worth minimums |
| SEC registration | Yes (public) | No (Reg D) | Public DPPs yes; private via Reg D |
| 1940 Act investment company? | No | No (exempt) | No |
| Management | Corporate management | GP/adviser | General partner |

## Chapter 8 quick review

- REIT: equity / mortgage / hybrid; distribute ≥ **90%** of income and ≥ 75% in real estate to avoid corporate tax; dividends taxed as ordinary income; **no loss pass-through**; listed REITs liquid, non-traded REITs illiquid with high fees; not a 1940 Act investment company.
- Hedge fund: private LP, Reg D, accredited investors only, lock-ups, 2-and-20 fees, leverage/shorting/derivatives, little disclosure; fund of hedge funds adds a layer of fees but can be sold to retail.
- DPP: limited partnership; GP manages with unlimited liability; LPs passive with limited liability (lose it if they manage); subscription agreement must be accepted by the GP; income **and losses** flow through (passive losses offset only passive income); depreciation/depletion/IDCs; **illiquid**; evaluate economic viability first; dissolution: secured creditors → general creditors → LPs → GP.
- Oil & gas: exploratory (riskiest), developmental, income (safest).
