# Chapter 10: Taxes

> Investment taxation is sprinkled throughout the exam — in questions about munis, Treasuries, mutual funds, retirement plans and gifts. This chapter consolidates the rules. Numbers below are the 2026 figures; the SIE tests concepts more than exact dollar thresholds.

---

## 10.1 Dividends

**Cash dividends** are taxable in the year received, whether taken in cash or reinvested.

- **Qualified dividends** — paid by US corporations (and qualifying foreign corporations) on stock held for more than 60 days around the ex-dividend date. Taxed at the **long-term capital gains rates (0%, 15% or 20%** depending on income).
- **Non-qualified (ordinary) dividends** — taxed as **ordinary income** at the investor's marginal rate. Includes dividends from REITs, money market funds, most bond fund "dividends" (which are really interest), and short-term capital gains distributions from mutual funds.
- **Stock dividends and stock splits** — **not taxable** when received; the investor's cost basis per share is adjusted downward (total basis spread over more shares). Tax applies only when the shares are sold.
- **Corporate recipients** — a corporation that receives dividends from another domestic corporation can exclude a portion (the **dividends-received deduction**, generally 50%, or 65% if it owns 20%+ of the payer) from taxable income. This is why preferred stock is popular with corporate investors.
- **Dividends from mutual funds** — reported on Form **1099-DIV**; long-term capital gains distributions are long-term to the shareholder no matter how long the fund shares were held.
- **Foreign dividends** — foreign taxes withheld can usually be claimed as a **foreign tax credit** on the US return.
- Brokerage firms report dividends to the IRS and investors on **Form 1099-DIV**; interest on **1099-INT**; sales proceeds and cost basis on **1099-B**.

## 10.2 Interest

| Source | Federal tax | State/local tax |
|---|---|---|
| **Corporate bonds**, CDs, bank accounts, money market funds, commercial paper | Taxable (ordinary income) | Taxable |
| **US Treasuries** (bills, notes, bonds, TIPS, STRIPS, savings bonds) and most **federal agencies** (FHLB, Farm Credit) | Taxable | **Exempt** |
| **Ginnie Mae, Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac** (mortgage-backed) | Taxable | Taxable |
| **Municipal bonds** | **Exempt** (except private activity bonds subject to AMT and taxable munis) | Exempt in the state of issue (usually); territory bonds exempt everywhere |

**Original issue discount (OID).** When a bond is issued below par (zero-coupon bonds are the extreme), the discount is treated as interest that **accretes** each year. For corporate and Treasury zeros, this accreted amount is **taxable each year** even though no cash is received ("**phantom income**"). The cost basis rises by the accretion, so there's no capital gain if held to maturity. For municipal OID, the accretion is tax-exempt interest. Zeros are therefore best held in tax-deferred accounts.

**Market discount.** A bond bought below par in the secondary market: the discount is taxed as **ordinary interest income** when the bond is sold or matures (or the investor may elect to accrue it annually). Applies even to munis.

**Premium bonds.** Taxable bonds bought above par: the investor may **amortize** the premium annually (reducing basis and reducing taxable interest). For tax-exempt munis, amortization is **required** and the amortized premium is **not deductible**.

**Accrued interest** paid to a seller is deducted from the buyer's first interest payment for tax purposes; the seller reports it as interest income.

**Series EE and I savings bonds** — interest is tax-deferred until redemption and may be entirely federally tax-free if used for qualified higher-education expenses (income limits apply).

## 10.3 Capital gains

A **capital gain or loss** is realized when a capital asset (stock, bond, fund share, option, real estate) is **sold** — not while it's merely appreciating ("unrealized" gains are not taxed).

```
Gain (loss) = Sale proceeds (net of commissions) − Cost basis
```

**Cost basis** = purchase price + commissions/fees paid (plus reinvested distributions for funds). Brokers report basis on Form 1099-B for covered securities.

**Holding period — the critical distinction.**

- **Short-term** — held **one year or less**. Taxed as **ordinary income**.
- **Long-term** — held **more than one year**. Taxed at the preferential rates of **0%, 15% or 20%** (plus a 3.8% net investment income tax for high earners).

The holding period starts the day **after** the trade date of the purchase and ends on the trade date of the sale.

**Netting rules.** Each year, short-term gains and losses are netted; long-term gains and losses are netted; then the two results are netted against each other.

- Net capital losses can offset capital gains without limit.
- After that, up to **$3,000** of net capital loss per year ($1,500 married filing separately) can be deducted against **ordinary income**.
- Any remaining loss is **carried forward** indefinitely to future years (keeping its short/long character).

**Example.** An investor has $10,000 of long-term gains and $18,000 of short-term losses. Net = $8,000 loss. $3,000 is deducted against ordinary income this year; $5,000 carries forward.

**Wash sale rule.** If an investor sells a security at a **loss** and buys the **same or a substantially identical** security within **30 days before or after** the sale (a 61-day window), the loss is **disallowed** for tax purposes. The disallowed loss is **added to the cost basis** of the new shares (it isn't lost forever, just deferred). "Substantially identical" includes the same stock, options or warrants on it, convertible bonds/preferred convertible into it, and the same mutual fund. Buying a different company in the same industry is **not** a wash sale. The rule doesn't apply to gains.

**Determining which shares were sold** (for partial sales of a position bought at different times/prices):

- **FIFO (first-in, first-out)** — the IRS default; usually produces the largest gain in a rising market.
- **Specific identification** — the investor tells the broker which lot to sell; most flexible for tax management (sell the highest-cost lot to minimize the gain).
- **Average cost** — permitted for mutual fund shares.

**Gifts and inheritances.**

- **Gifted securities:** the recipient takes the donor's **cost basis and holding period** (carryover basis). Exception: if the stock has declined below the donor's basis at the time of the gift, the recipient's basis for a loss is the fair market value at the gift date. The donor may give up to the **annual gift tax exclusion (2026: $19,000 per recipient; $38,000 for a married couple)** without filing a gift tax return; larger gifts use up part of the lifetime estate/gift exemption. Gifts between spouses are unlimited and untaxed.
- **Inherited securities:** the heir's basis is **stepped up (or down) to the fair market value on the date of death**, and the holding period is **automatically long-term** regardless of how long the deceased or heir held it. This "step-up" erases the unrealized gain. The estate may owe estate tax if it exceeds the exemption (2026: about $15 million per person).

**Other capital-gain items.**

- **Mutual fund exchanges** within a family are taxable sales.
- **Bond conversion** to stock is not a taxable event.
- **Options:** expired options are capital gains/losses; exercised options adjust the stock's basis or proceeds (Chapter 9).
- **Broad-based index options, futures:** 60% long-term / 40% short-term regardless of holding period.
- **Collectibles** (precious metal coins, art) long-term gains are taxed at a maximum 28%.
- **Return of capital** distributions (common with REITs, MLPs, some funds) are not taxed when received; they **reduce cost basis**.

## 10.4 Types of income

The tax code sorts income into three buckets, and losses from one bucket generally can't offset income in another.

| Type | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Earned (active) income** | Wages, salaries, tips, bonuses, self-employment income | Taxed at ordinary rates; the only income eligible for **IRA contributions**; subject to payroll (FICA) taxes |
| **Portfolio (investment) income** | Interest, dividends, capital gains, royalties | Interest and non-qualified dividends taxed as ordinary income; qualified dividends and LT gains at preferential rates; capital losses offset portfolio gains (plus $3,000 of ordinary income) |
| **Passive income** | Income from **limited partnerships/DPPs**, rental real estate the investor doesn't actively manage, businesses in which the investor doesn't materially participate | **Passive losses can offset only passive income**, never earned or portfolio income; unused losses carry forward until the activity is sold |

**Progressive taxation.** The US income tax is **progressive** — higher income is taxed at higher **marginal rates** (brackets currently range from 10% to 37%). The **marginal rate** is the rate on the next dollar earned; the **effective rate** is total tax ÷ total income. **Regressive** taxes (sales tax, FICA up to the wage base) take a larger percentage of lower incomes. A **flat tax** charges one rate to everyone.

**Alternative minimum tax (AMT).** A parallel calculation that adds back certain "preference items" (including interest on **private activity municipal bonds**, accelerated depreciation, certain DPP deductions) to make sure high-income taxpayers pay a minimum amount. Investors subject to AMT should avoid private activity bonds.

**Tax-advantaged vs. tax-deferred vs. tax-free.**

- **Tax-deferred** — no tax now, taxed later (traditional IRA/401(k), annuity gains, savings bond interest). Growth compounds untaxed.
- **Tax-free (tax-exempt)** — never taxed (Roth IRA qualified withdrawals, municipal bond interest, 529 qualified withdrawals).
- **Tax-advantaged** — umbrella term for both.

**Withholding and reporting.**

- Brokers must obtain a customer's **Social Security/taxpayer ID number** (Form W-9); if not provided, the firm must apply **backup withholding (24%)** on dividends, interest and sales proceeds.
- Foreign investors complete Form **W-8BEN**; US tax is withheld on dividends (often 30% or a treaty rate) but generally not on capital gains or portfolio interest.
- Tax year for individuals is the **calendar year**; returns due April 15. Securities sold by December 31 (trade date) count for that year, even though settlement may occur in January.

**Tax strategies the exam may describe.**

- **Tax-loss harvesting** — selling losers to offset gains (mind the wash sale rule).
- **Asset location** — hold tax-inefficient assets (taxable bonds, REITs, zeros) in tax-deferred accounts and tax-efficient assets (index funds, munis) in taxable accounts.
- **Holding past one year** to convert short-term to long-term treatment.
- **Gifting appreciated stock** to charity (deduct FMV, avoid the gain) or to lower-bracket family members (subject to "kiddie tax" rules for children's unearned income).

## Chapter 10 quick review

- Qualified dividends & long-term gains: 0/15/20%. Ordinary dividends, interest, short-term gains: ordinary rates. Stock dividends/splits: not taxable; basis adjusted.
- Treasuries: federal tax yes, state no. Munis: federal no, in-state usually no; private activity bonds → AMT. GNMA/FNMA/FHLMC: taxed at all levels.
- OID accretion on corporate/Treasury zeros is taxed annually (phantom income); muni OID is tax-free; market discount is ordinary income; muni premium must be amortized.
- Long-term = **more than one year**. Net losses: offset gains, then **$3,000**/yr vs. ordinary income, carry forward the rest.
- **Wash sale:** loss disallowed if the same/substantially identical security is bought within **30 days before or after**; loss added to the new basis.
- Default lot method: FIFO; specific ID is most flexible; average cost allowed for funds.
- **Gift: donor's basis carries over.** **Inheritance: stepped-up basis to date-of-death value, automatically long-term.** Annual gift exclusion $19,000 (2026).
- Earned income is required for IRA contributions. Passive losses offset only passive income.
- Progressive tax = higher brackets at higher income. Backup withholding 24% if no TIN. Trade date governs the tax year.
