## 5.1 Foundations & taxation

**Who issues municipal bonds ("munis").** States, cities, counties, towns, school districts, public authorities (airports, toll roads, water utilities), and US territories (Puerto Rico, Guam, US Virgin Islands). The federal government does **not** issue munis.

**Why they exist.** To finance public projects — schools, roads, bridges, hospitals, sewer systems — and sometimes to fund operations.

**The tax advantage — the whole reason people buy munis.**

- Interest on most municipal bonds is **exempt from federal income tax**.
- If the investor lives in the state of issuance, interest is usually also exempt from **state and local** tax ("double" or "triple" tax-exempt).
- Bonds issued by **US territories** (Puerto Rico, etc.) are **triple tax-exempt for all US investors** regardless of state.
- **Capital gains** on munis are fully taxable — only the *interest* is exempt. Buy a muni at $950, sell at $1,000 → $50 taxable capital gain.
- **Private activity bonds** (munis that mainly benefit a private entity, e.g., a stadium or industrial development bond) may be subject to the **alternative minimum tax (AMT)**.
- Interest on municipal bonds is **not exempt** if the bond is a "taxable muni" (e.g., Build America Bonds, some pension-funding bonds).

**Tax-equivalent yield (TEY).** The taxable yield an investor would need to match a muni's tax-free yield.

```
Tax-equivalent yield = Municipal yield ÷ (1 − Tax bracket)
```

**Example.** A 4% muni for an investor in the 32% bracket: 4% ÷ (1 − 0.32) = 4% ÷ 0.68 = **5.88%**. A corporate bond would need to yield more than 5.88% to be better after tax.

Reverse direction: **tax-free equivalent yield** = corporate yield × (1 − tax bracket). A 6% corporate at a 32% bracket ≈ 4.08% tax-free.

**Rule:** the **higher** the investor's tax bracket, the **more** attractive munis are. Munis are **inappropriate** inside tax-deferred retirement accounts (IRAs, 401(k)s) — the tax benefit is wasted and muni yields are lower than taxable yields.

**Legal opinion.** Every new municipal issue carries a **legal opinion** from bond counsel stating that the bond is a valid, legally binding obligation of the issuer and that the interest is tax-exempt. An **unqualified** legal opinion has no reservations (good); a **qualified** opinion raises concerns.

**Regulation.** Municipal securities are **exempt from registration** under the Securities Act of 1933 and from the Trust Indenture Act. Instead of a prospectus, the issuer prepares an **official statement (OS)**; a **preliminary official statement** is used during marketing. The **Municipal Securities Rulemaking Board (MSRB)** writes rules for dealers and advisers in munis but has **no enforcement power** — FINRA, the SEC and bank regulators enforce MSRB rules. The **anti-fraud** provisions of federal securities law still apply fully to munis. The **EMMA** website (Electronic Municipal Market Access) publishes official statements, continuing disclosures and trade data.

## 5.2 General obligation (G.O.) bonds

**Backed by the full faith, credit and taxing power of the issuer.** Repaid from general tax revenues.

- State G.O.s are backed mainly by **income and sales taxes**.
- Local G.O.s (cities, counties, school districts) are backed mainly by **property taxes (ad valorem taxes)**, assessed in **mills** (1 mill = $1 per $1,000 of assessed value, or 0.1%).
- Because they pledge the taxing power, G.O.s usually require **voter approval** and are subject to **statutory debt limits** (a cap on how much the issuer can borrow).
- Generally **safer** than revenue bonds from the same issuer (lower yield).

**Analyzing a G.O. bond** (credit factors):

- Tax base and its diversity; property values; population trends.
- Existing debt per capita, overlapping debt (debt of other overlapping jurisdictions on the same taxpayers).
- Budget practices, unfunded pension liabilities.
- Overall economy of the municipality.

**Double-barreled bonds.** Revenue bonds that are *also* backed by the issuer's taxing power if revenues fall short. Rated as G.O.s.

## 5.3 Revenue bonds

**Backed only by the revenue of a specific project or source**, not by taxes. Examples: toll roads and bridges, airports, water and sewer systems, hospitals, public power utilities, stadiums, housing. If the project doesn't generate enough revenue, bondholders are not paid — the issuer's tax base is not pledged.

- **No voter approval** required and **not subject to debt limits** (because taxes aren't pledged).
- Generally **higher yield** than G.O.s (more risk).
- **Feasibility study** — prepared by engineers/consultants to project whether the project will generate enough revenue; a key document for analysis.
- **Rate covenant** — issuer promises to charge user fees high enough to cover expenses and debt service.
- **Debt service coverage ratio** = net revenue available ÷ annual debt service (principal + interest). A ratio of 2.0 means revenue is twice what's needed; higher is safer.
- **Flow of funds** — the indenture specifies the order in which revenues are applied: operating & maintenance expenses first, then debt service, then reserve funds, then surplus.
- Other covenants: insurance covenant, maintenance covenant, additional-bonds test (limits new debt with equal claim), non-discrimination covenant.

**Special types of revenue bonds.**

| Type | Backed by |
|---|---|
| **Industrial development (IDR/IDB)** | Lease payments from a private corporation using the facility; credit depends on the corporation; usually subject to AMT (private activity bond) |
| **Special tax bonds** | A specific tax (e.g., gasoline, tobacco, hotel tax) |
| **Special assessment bonds** | Assessments on properties that benefit from the improvement (sidewalks, sewers) |
| **Moral obligation bonds** | Project revenue, plus a non-binding promise that the state legislature *may* appropriate funds if needed |
| **Lease-rental bonds** | Lease payments from a government entity that uses the facility (schools, office buildings) |
| **Certificates of participation (COPs)** | Lease revenues; avoid voter approval requirements |
| **Housing bonds** | Mortgage payments; often federally backed |
| **Public housing authority (PHA/NHA) bonds** | Backed by the US government — safest muni |
| **Double-barreled** | Project revenue plus taxing power |

## 5.4 Short-term notes

Municipalities issue short-term notes (typically **less than 12 months**, sometimes up to 3 years) to bridge timing gaps between spending and incoming revenue, or as interim financing before a bond sale. Notes are rated separately (Moody's MIG 1–3 for notes; VMIG for variable-rate demand notes; S&P SP-1 to SP-3).

| Note | Repaid from |
|---|---|
| **Tax anticipation notes (TANs)** | Future tax collections |
| **Revenue anticipation notes (RANs)** | Future non-tax revenue (e.g., state or federal aid) |
| **Tax and revenue anticipation notes (TRANs)** | Both |
| **Bond anticipation notes (BANs)** | Proceeds of a future long-term bond issue |
| **Grant anticipation notes (GANs)** | Expected federal/state grant money |
| **Construction loan notes (CLNs)** | Permanent financing after a housing project is built |

**Variable-rate demand obligations (VRDOs) / tax-exempt commercial paper** — longer-term munis with rates that reset periodically (daily/weekly) and a **put** feature letting investors sell back at par on reset dates; treated as money-market instruments. **Auction rate securities (ARS)** are similar but reset via auctions and became illiquid in 2008 when auctions failed.

**Tax-exempt money market funds** invest in these instruments.

## 5.5 Trading

**Primary market.** Most new munis are sold through **underwriting syndicates**:

- **Competitive bid** — the issuer publishes a **notice of sale** and awards the issue to the syndicate offering the lowest net interest cost (NIC) or true interest cost (TIC). Typical for **G.O.** bonds.
- **Negotiated** — the issuer chooses an underwriter and negotiates terms. Typical for **revenue** bonds.
- The syndicate signs a **syndicate letter / agreement among underwriters**. Orders are filled in priority: **pre-sale → group net → designated → member**.
- Buyers receive the **official statement**; MSRB rules require delivery of a final OS by settlement.

**Secondary market.** Munis trade **OTC** through dealers. The market is thin; many issues rarely trade. Features:

- Quotes are typically on a **yield (basis) for serial bonds** and on **price for term bonds**.
- Trades are reported to the MSRB's **Real-Time Transaction Reporting System (RTRS)** within 15 minutes and displayed on EMMA.
- Settlement: **T+1** (regular way). Accrued interest on a **30/360** basis.
- Dealers may act as broker (agent) or dealer (principal); **MSRB Rule G-30** requires fair prices and markups, and markup disclosure on retail confirmations.
- **Broker's brokers** — intermediaries that help dealers find buyers/sellers of munis without revealing identities.
- **Municipal bond insurance** — issuers can buy insurance (from AGM, BAM, etc.) guaranteeing principal and interest; insured bonds carry the insurer's rating.
- **Bank-qualified bonds** — small G.O. issues (≤ $10 million/year) that banks can buy and deduct 80% of the carrying cost.

**Key MSRB rules to know.**

| Rule | Topic |
|---|---|
| G-17 | Fair dealing; dealers must deal fairly with everyone |
| G-19 | Suitability |
| G-20 | Gifts and gratuities — **$100 per person per year** limit (business entertainment excluded) |
| G-30 | Fair pricing (markups) |
| G-32 | Delivery of official statement by settlement |
| G-37 | **Political contributions / "pay-to-play"** — an MFP may give at most **$250 per election** to a candidate the MFP **is entitled to vote for**; a larger contribution, or any contribution to an official the MFP can't vote for, bars the dealer from negotiated underwriting with that issuer for **two years** |
| G-39 | Telemarketing |

> **G-37 clarified:** An MFP may give up to **$250 per election** to a candidate **for whom the MFP is eligible to vote**. Any contribution above that, or *any* contribution to an official the MFP can't vote for, triggers a **two-year ban** on the firm doing negotiated municipal business with that issuer.

**Tax treatment of discounts and premiums.**

- **Original issue discount (OID)** munis — the discount accretes tax-free (treated as tax-exempt interest).
- **Market discount** munis (bought at a discount in the secondary market) — the discount is taxed as **ordinary income** when realized.
- **Premium** munis — the premium must be **amortized** (reduces cost basis); the amortization is not deductible because the interest is tax-free.

## 5.6 Suitability

**Ideal investor:** high federal tax bracket, seeking **tax-free income**, investing in a **taxable account**, often a resident of the issuing state (for state tax exemption). Conservative to moderate risk tolerance.

**Not appropriate for:**

- Low-bracket investors — a taxable bond will usually give more after tax. Always compare using the tax-equivalent yield.
- **Tax-deferred accounts** (IRAs, 401(k)s) — the tax exemption is wasted.
- Investors seeking growth.
- Corporations are rarely good buyers (lower yields; corporate rates) unless in high brackets.

**Risk factors specific to munis:**

- **Credit risk** varies — G.O.s of large states are very safe; small revenue bonds and territory bonds (Puerto Rico's default) can be risky.
- **Legislative risk** — a change in the tax code could reduce the value of the exemption.
- **Liquidity risk** — thin secondary market.
- **Call risk** — most munis are callable (typically 10 years after issue).
- **Interest-rate risk** — like all bonds.

**Muni bond funds and ETFs** offer diversification and are appropriate for investors who want tax-free income but can't buy enough individual bonds to diversify.

## Chapter 5 quick review

- Issued by states, cities, authorities, territories; interest exempt from **federal** tax; state exemption usually only for in-state residents; territory bonds exempt everywhere.
- Capital gains on munis are taxable. Private activity bonds may trigger AMT.
- **TEY = muni yield ÷ (1 − tax bracket).** Higher bracket → munis more attractive. Never in an IRA.
- **G.O.**: taxing power, voter approval, debt limits, property (ad valorem) taxes for local, safer.
- **Revenue**: project revenue only, no voter approval, feasibility study, rate covenant, coverage ratio, flow of funds, higher yield. Variants: IDRs (private corp, AMT), special tax, special assessment, moral obligation, double-barreled, PHA (US-backed, safest).
- Notes: TANs, RANs, TRANs, BANs, GANs; VRDOs have a put feature.
- Exempt from 1933 Act registration — official statement, legal opinion (unqualified is good). MSRB makes rules; FINRA/SEC enforce; EMMA for disclosure.
- Competitive bid for G.O.s (notice of sale), negotiated for revenue; order priority pre-sale → group net → designated → member.
- MSRB G-37: $250 limit to candidates you can vote for; otherwise two-year ban. G-20: $100 gift limit.
- Settlement T+1; 30/360 accrued interest; RTRS trade reporting.
