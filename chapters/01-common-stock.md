# Chapter 1: Common Stock

> **Exam weight context:** Equity securities fall under FINRA SIE Section 2, "Understanding Products and Their Risks" (44% of the exam, the largest section). Expect several questions on stockholder rights, dividends, settlement, short sales, ADRs and basic fundamental analysis.

---

## 1.1 Basic characteristics

**What common stock is.** Common stock represents an *ownership* (equity) interest in a corporation. When a company incorporates, its charter authorizes a maximum number of shares (**authorized shares**). Shares actually sold to investors are **issued shares**. Shares the company later buys back and holds are **treasury stock**. Shares still in investors' hands are **outstanding shares**:

```
Outstanding shares = Issued shares − Treasury stock
```

Treasury stock has no voting rights and receives no dividends.

**Par value.** An arbitrary accounting value (often $0.01 or $1). It has nothing to do with market value. For common stock, par is essentially meaningless to investors; for preferred stock and bonds, par is critical (see later chapters).

**Limited liability.** The most a common stockholder can lose is the amount invested. Shareholders are never liable for the corporation's debts.

**Why companies issue stock.** To raise capital without taking on debt. Unlike bond interest, dividends are never a legal obligation, so stock is a flexible funding source — but it dilutes existing owners.

**Why investors buy it.**

- **Capital appreciation** (growth) — the primary reason. Historically, stocks have outperformed bonds and cash over long periods.
- **Dividend income** — paid at the discretion of the board of directors.
- **Inflation hedge** — because stock prices and corporate earnings tend to rise with prices in the economy.

**Risks of common stock.**

| Risk | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Market (systematic) risk | The whole market falls and takes the stock with it. Cannot be diversified away. |
| Business (non-systematic) risk | The specific company does poorly. *Can* be reduced by diversification. |
| Liquidity risk | Difficulty selling at a fair price (thinly traded stocks). |
| Legislative/regulatory risk | New laws hurt the company or industry. |
| Currency risk | Relevant for foreign stocks and ADRs. |
| Liquidation risk | Common stockholders are paid **last** if the company goes bankrupt. |

**Market capitalization.** Market cap = share price × shares outstanding. Common buckets: large-cap (roughly $10B+), mid-cap ($2B–$10B), small-cap (under $2B), micro-cap. Smaller caps = more growth potential but more volatility and risk.

**Types of common stock by investment style.**

- **Growth stocks** — reinvest earnings, pay little or no dividend, high P/E ratios.
- **Value stocks** — trade below what fundamentals suggest; often pay dividends.
- **Income stocks** — mature companies with reliable, high dividends (utilities).
- **Blue chips** — large, well-established, financially sound companies.
- **Cyclical** — earnings rise and fall with the economy (autos, airlines, steel).
- **Defensive (non-cyclical)** — stable regardless of the economy (food, utilities, pharmaceuticals, tobacco).
- **Speculative / penny stocks** — high risk. A **penny stock** is a non-exchange-listed stock trading under **$5 per share**; special disclosure and suitability rules apply (the "penny stock rules," SEC Rule 15g-9).

## 1.2 Rights of common stockholders

Common stockholders are owners, and ownership brings a bundle of rights. Memorize these seven.

### 1.2.1 Pro-rata share of dividends

If the board declares a dividend, every share of the same class gets the same amount. A shareholder with 1% of the shares gets 1% of the total dividend. Dividends are *not* guaranteed — the board can suspend or cut them at any time. Dividends can be paid in cash, in additional stock (stock dividend), or occasionally in property/shares of a subsidiary.

### 1.2.2 Board of Directors

Common stockholders elect the board, which hires management and sets dividend policy. Stockholders vote on major corporate matters (mergers, issuance of new shares beyond authorization, stock splits, changes to the charter). They do **not** vote on whether to pay a dividend — that's the board's decision.

Two voting methods:

- **Statutory voting** — one vote per share per board seat. If you own 100 shares and 5 seats are open, you can cast up to 100 votes *for each* seat. Favors large shareholders.
- **Cumulative voting** — total votes = shares × seats open (100 × 5 = 500), which you may distribute however you want, including all 500 on one candidate. Favors **small/minority shareholders** because they can concentrate their votes.

Shareholders who cannot attend the annual meeting vote by **proxy** (a written authorization for someone else to vote their shares). Proxy solicitations are regulated by the SEC under the Securities Exchange Act of 1934.

### 1.2.3 Inspection of books and records

Stockholders can inspect certain corporate records — the annual report, audited financial statements, and the list of shareholders. This does not extend to day-to-day internal records.

### 1.2.4 Maintaining proportionate ownership

The **preemptive right**: when the corporation issues new shares, existing stockholders get the first chance to buy enough to maintain their percentage of ownership. This is delivered through a **rights offering**.

**Rights (subscription rights):**

- Short-term (typically 30–60 days).
- One right per share owned; a set number of rights plus cash buys one new share.
- The subscription price is **below the current market price**, so rights have value and trade in the secondary market.
- A shareholder can exercise, sell, or let them expire.

**Warrants** (for comparison — frequently tested next to rights):

- Long-term (years, sometimes perpetual).
- Exercise price is **above** the market price at issuance; they have no intrinsic value initially and are a long-term speculative bet.
- Usually attached to a bond or preferred offering as a "sweetener" to lower the issuer's borrowing cost. Can be detached and traded separately.

| Feature | Rights | Warrants |
|---|---|---|
| Term | Short (weeks) | Long (years) |
| Exercise price vs. market | Below | Above |
| Who receives | Existing shareholders | Usually bond/preferred buyers as sweetener |
| Purpose | Preemptive right | Sweetener |

### 1.2.5 Stock splits

Shareholders vote on splits. A split changes the number of shares and the price per share, but **not** the total market value of a holding or the shareholder's ownership percentage.

- **Forward split** (e.g., 2-for-1): more shares, lower price. 100 shares @ $80 → 200 shares @ $40. Done to make shares more affordable/liquid.
- **Reverse split** (e.g., 1-for-10): fewer shares, higher price. 1,000 shares @ $0.50 → 100 shares @ $5. Done to avoid exchange delisting or improve image.

Quick method for any split "A-for-B": new shares = old shares × A/B; new price = old price × B/A.

A **stock dividend** (e.g., 10%) works like a small forward split: 100 shares @ $50 → 110 shares @ $45.45. Total value unchanged; cost basis per share is adjusted down.

### 1.2.6 Assets upon liquidation

If the corporation is liquidated, common stockholders have a **residual claim** — they are paid last, after:

1. Secured creditors (bondholders with collateral)
2. Administrative costs, unpaid wages and taxes
3. Unsecured creditors / general creditors (debentures, suppliers)
4. Subordinated debenture holders
5. Preferred stockholders
6. **Common stockholders**

This is why common stock is the riskiest security a corporation issues (junior-most claim).

### 1.2.7 Transfer ownership

Shares are **freely transferable** (negotiable) — the holder can sell or gift them without the issuer's permission. Transfers are recorded by the **transfer agent** (maintains the shareholder list, issues/cancels certificates, pays dividends) and audited by the **registrar** (makes sure shares outstanding never exceed authorized). Most shares today are held in **street name** (registered to the broker-dealer, beneficially owned by the customer) or in book-entry form at the **Depository Trust Company (DTC)**.

## 1.3 Trading

### 1.3.1 Negotiable or redeemable

A **negotiable** security is one you sell to *another investor* in the secondary market — common stock, bonds, closed-end funds, ETFs. A **redeemable** security is one you sell back to the *issuer* — open-end mutual funds, unit investment trusts, variable annuities. Common stock is negotiable: the corporation does not redeem it (though it may voluntarily buy shares back).

### 1.3.2 The primary & secondary market

- **Primary market** — the issuer sells new securities to investors (IPO, follow-on offering). Proceeds go to the **issuer**. Governed mainly by the Securities Act of 1933.
- **Secondary market** — investors trade existing securities among themselves (NYSE, Nasdaq, OTC). Proceeds go to the **selling investor**, not the issuer. Governed mainly by the Securities Exchange Act of 1934.

The secondary market is further divided into the first market (exchange-listed on exchange), second market (OTC/unlisted), third market (listed stocks traded OTC), and fourth market (institution-to-institution directly, via ECNs). See Chapter 12.

### 1.3.3 Settlement

Settlement is when the buyer's money and the seller's security are actually exchanged.

| Security | Regular-way settlement |
|---|---|
| Corporate stocks & bonds, municipal bonds, ETFs, mutual funds | **T+1** (one business day after trade date) |
| US Treasury securities, options | **T+1** |
| Cash settlement | Same day |
| Reg T payment deadline (broker-dealer customer) | **T+3** (two business days after settlement, i.e., S+2) |

> The US moved corporate securities from T+2 to **T+1 on May 28, 2024**. The exam tests T+1.

If a customer fails to pay by the Reg T deadline, the firm must sell out the position and **freeze** the account for 90 days (cash must be in the account before any purchase).

### 1.3.4 Cash dividends

Four dates to memorize, in order:

1. **Declaration date** — board announces the dividend, the amount, the record date and the payable date.
2. **Ex-dividend date** — first day the stock trades *without* the dividend. Set by the exchange/FINRA. With T+1 settlement, **ex-date = record date** (same day). Buyers on or after the ex-date do not get the dividend. On the ex-date the stock's opening price is reduced by the dividend amount.
3. **Record date** — the issuer checks its books; shareholders of record receive the dividend.
4. **Payable date** — dividend is paid.

Mnemonic: **DERP** (Declaration, Ex, Record, Payable).

To receive the dividend you must *buy before* the ex-date (so that the trade settles by the record date). A buyer who purchases on the ex-date or later does not receive it; the seller does.

**Dividend yield** = annual dividend ÷ current market price. A stock at $40 paying $0.50 quarterly has a yield of $2.00 / $40 = 5%.

Cash dividends are taxable in the year received. Qualified dividends get lower long-term capital gains rates (Chapter 10).

### 1.3.5 Selling short

A **short sale** is selling stock you do not own by borrowing it (from the broker-dealer's inventory or other customers' margin accounts) and hoping to buy it back later at a lower price.

- Profit if the price falls; loss if it rises.
- **Maximum gain** = sale price × shares (stock goes to zero). **Maximum loss = unlimited** (no ceiling on how high a stock can go). This is why short selling is suitable only for sophisticated/risk-tolerant investors.
- Must be done in a **margin account**.
- The short seller must pay any dividends to the lender of the shares.
- Regulation SHO: broker must *locate* borrowable shares before executing a short sale; "naked" shorting is prohibited. Short sale orders must be marked "short."
- Short interest is reported by exchanges; a **short squeeze** occurs when a rising price forces shorts to buy back, pushing the price even higher.

### 1.3.6 American Depositary Receipts

An **ADR** is a negotiable receipt issued by a US bank representing shares of a foreign company held in custody abroad. ADRs let US investors buy foreign stocks easily:

- Trade on US exchanges or OTC, **priced and paying dividends in US dollars**.
- The holder bears **currency (exchange-rate) risk** — if the foreign currency weakens against the dollar, the ADR's value and dividends fall even if the foreign share price is unchanged.
- ADR holders generally do **not** have voting rights (the depositary bank holds the actual shares).
- The foreign country may withhold tax on dividends; the US investor can often claim a foreign tax credit.
- Sponsored ADRs are issued with the company's cooperation (can list on NYSE/Nasdaq); unsponsored ADRs trade OTC.

### 1.3.7 Tender offers & buybacks

- **Tender offer** — an offer, usually at a premium to market, to buy shares directly from stockholders (often part of a takeover attempt). Must remain open at least **20 business days**. Shareholders may tender only shares they actually own ("net long" position) — you cannot tender borrowed/short shares.
- **Buyback (share repurchase)** — the company buys its own shares in the open market, converting outstanding shares into treasury stock. Fewer shares outstanding → higher earnings per share and often a higher price. An alternative to dividends for returning cash to shareholders.

## 1.4 Suitability

Common stock is suitable for investors whose primary objective is **growth (capital appreciation)** with a long time horizon. Specific matches:

| Investor profile | Appropriate common stock type |
|---|---|
| Young investor, long horizon, aggressive | Growth, small-cap, speculative |
| Needs current income but wants equity exposure | Income/blue-chip/utility stocks |
| Conservative, wants stability | Blue-chip, defensive |
| Expecting economic expansion | Cyclical stocks |
| Expecting recession | Defensive stocks |
| Wants international diversification | ADRs, foreign stocks (accept currency risk) |
| Bearish on a specific stock, risk tolerant | Short sale (margin account required) |

Common stock is **not** appropriate as a primary holding for investors who need principal preservation or who have a very short time horizon, because of market volatility.

## 1.5 Fundamental analysis

Fundamental analysts study a company's financial statements and economic position to determine what the stock *should* be worth. (Contrast with **technical analysts**, who study price and volume charts — trendlines, support/resistance, head-and-shoulders patterns, moving averages — and ignore fundamentals.)

**The balance sheet** — a snapshot at a point in time:

```
Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity (net worth)
```

- Current assets (cash, receivables, inventory — convertible to cash within a year) vs. fixed assets.
- Current liabilities (due within a year) vs. long-term debt.
- **Working capital** = current assets − current liabilities (measures liquidity).
- **Current ratio** = current assets ÷ current liabilities.
- **Quick (acid-test) ratio** = (current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities.
- **Debt-to-equity ratio** = long-term debt ÷ shareholders' equity (measures leverage).
- **Book value per share** = (shareholders' equity − preferred stock) ÷ common shares outstanding.

**The income statement** — performance over a period:

```
Revenue − Cost of goods sold = Gross profit
Gross profit − Operating expenses = Operating income (EBIT)
EBIT − Interest = Pre-tax income
Pre-tax income − Taxes = Net income
Net income − Preferred dividends = Earnings available to common
```

- **Earnings per share (EPS)** = earnings available to common ÷ common shares outstanding.
- **Price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio** = market price ÷ EPS. High P/E → growth stock / high expectations; low P/E → value stock.
- **Dividend payout ratio** = dividends per share ÷ EPS.
- **Dividend yield** = annual dividend ÷ market price.
- **Return on equity** = net income ÷ shareholders' equity.

**Example.** A company earns $5 million, pays $1 million in preferred dividends, has 2 million common shares, and trades at $40. EPS = ($5M − $1M) / 2M = $2.00. P/E = $40 / $2 = 20.

**Other fundamental considerations:** management quality, competitive position, industry trends, and macro factors (interest rates, GDP, inflation). The efficient market hypothesis (EMH) argues that prices already reflect all available information — strong form says even insider information is priced in, semi-strong says all public information, weak form says only past prices.

## Chapter 1 quick review

- Common stock = ownership; limited liability; paid **last** in liquidation.
- Outstanding = issued − treasury.
- Seven rights: dividends pro-rata, vote for board (statutory vs. cumulative — cumulative helps small holders), inspect books, preemptive rights (rights offering, short-term, below market), vote on splits, residual assets, free transferability.
- Rights are short/below market; warrants are long/above market/sweeteners.
- Splits change share count and price, not total value or ownership %.
- Settlement: **T+1** for stocks, bonds, options, Treasuries. Reg T payment: S+2 (T+3).
- Dividend dates: Declaration → Ex (= record date under T+1) → Record → Payable. Buy *before* ex-date to get the dividend.
- Short sale: borrowed stock, margin account, unlimited loss, locate requirement (Reg SHO).
- ADR: foreign stock, US dollars, currency risk, usually no vote.
- Tender offer open ≥ 20 business days; only tender shares you own.
- Fundamental vs. technical analysis; EPS, P/E, current ratio, book value, debt/equity.
