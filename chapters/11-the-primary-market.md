# Chapter 11: The Primary Market

> How new securities come to market: the players, underwriting commitments, types of offerings, the IPO process under the Securities Act of 1933, exemptions (Reg D, Reg A, Rule 147, 144A), Rule 144 for restricted/control stock, and the related FINRA rules (Rule 5130 on IPO allocations).

---

## 11.1 Roles

The **primary market** is where an issuer sells new securities to investors for the first time and **receives the proceeds**. The transaction is governed by the **Securities Act of 1933** (the "Paper Act" / "Truth in Securities Act"), whose goals are full and fair disclosure (via registration and the prospectus) and the prevention of fraud in new issues.

**Key participants.**

| Participant | Role |
|---|---|
| **Issuer** | The corporation, municipality or government selling the securities to raise capital |
| **Investment banker / underwriter** | A broker-dealer that advises the issuer, helps structure and price the offering, and distributes the securities to investors |
| **Managing (lead) underwriter / syndicate manager** | The firm that leads the deal, forms the syndicate, runs the books, and handles the stabilizing bid |
| **Syndicate** | A group of underwriters that share the risk and distribution; bound by the **agreement among underwriters (syndicate agreement)** |
| **Selling group** | Broker-dealers that help sell the issue but take **no underwriting risk** (act as agents for a concession) |
| **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** | Reviews the registration statement for **completeness** (not merit); never approves or guarantees an offering |
| **Transfer agent / registrar** | Issue and record ownership of the new shares |
| **Legal counsel, auditors** | Due diligence, legal opinions, audited financials |

**The underwriting spread** — the difference between the public offering price and what the issuer receives — is split into:

- **Manager's fee** (smallest piece, to the lead for running the deal),
- **Underwriting (syndicate) fee** (to all syndicate members for taking risk),
- **Selling concession** (largest piece, to whoever sells the shares — syndicate members or the selling group).

A selling group member earns only the concession; a syndicate member that sells earns the underwriting fee plus the concession; the manager earns all three on shares it sells.

## 11.2 Underwriting commitments

The underwriting agreement between the issuer and the syndicate specifies how much risk the underwriters take.

**Firm commitment** — the underwriters **buy the entire issue from the issuer** and resell it to the public. They act as **principals** and bear the full risk of unsold shares. The issuer is guaranteed its money. This is the most common arrangement for corporate IPOs and most negotiated offerings.

- **Standby** underwriting — a form of firm commitment used with a **rights offering**: the underwriter agrees to buy any shares existing shareholders don't subscribe for.

**Best efforts** — the underwriters act as **agents**, selling as much as they can and returning unsold shares to the issuer. No risk of ownership for the underwriter; the issuer bears the risk that not all shares sell. Variations:

- **All-or-none (AON)** — the entire issue must sell or the offering is cancelled and investors' money is returned. Funds are held in escrow until completion.
- **Mini-max** — a minimum amount must be sold for the deal to go forward; the underwriter may then sell up to a maximum. Also escrow-based.

| | Firm commitment | Best efforts |
|---|---|---|
| Underwriter's capacity | Principal (dealer) | Agent (broker) |
| Who bears unsold-share risk | Underwriter | Issuer |
| Underwriter's compensation | Spread | Commission/concession |
| Typical use | Established issuers, IPOs | Smaller/riskier issuers, DPPs |

**Competitive vs. negotiated.** In a **competitive bid** (used mostly for municipal G.O. bonds and utilities), syndicates submit sealed bids and the issuer picks the lowest cost; in a **negotiated** underwriting (corporates, revenue bonds), the issuer selects the underwriter and they negotiate terms.

**Stabilization.** During the offering, the managing underwriter may enter a **stabilizing bid** at or **below** the public offering price (never above) to support the price and prevent a collapse while the issue is being distributed. This is the only legal form of price manipulation, and it must be disclosed in the prospectus.

**Green shoe (over-allotment option).** Allows underwriters to sell up to **15%** more shares than planned if demand is strong.

## 11.3 Types of offerings

| Offering | Description |
|---|---|
| **Initial public offering (IPO)** | A company's first sale of stock to the public; the company goes from private to public |
| **Follow-on / subsequent (additional) public offering** | Sale of new shares by a company that is already public |
| **Primary offering** | The **issuer** sells newly created shares; proceeds go to the **issuer** (raises capital). Both IPOs and follow-ons are primary offerings. |
| **Secondary offering** | **Existing shareholders** (founders, VCs, insiders) sell their shares in a registered offering; proceeds go to the **selling shareholders**, not the company. No new shares are created. (Note: "secondary offering" ≠ "secondary market.") |
| **Split (combined) offering** | Part primary, part secondary |
| **Shelf registration (Rule 415)** | A seasoned issuer registers securities once and sells them over up to **3 years** "off the shelf" as market conditions warrant, with a supplement to the base prospectus for each takedown |
| **Rights offering** | New shares offered to existing shareholders first (preemptive rights); may be backed by a standby underwriter |
| **Private placement** | Unregistered sale to a limited number of accredited/sophisticated investors under Reg D (see 11.4.4) |
| **Direct listing** | A company lists existing shares on an exchange without an underwritten offering (no new capital raised) |
| **SPAC** | A "blank check" shell company that IPOs to raise cash and later merges with a private company, taking it public |
| **Tender offer / exchange offer** | Offer to buy back shares or swap securities (not a capital raise) |

## 11.4 The IPO process

### 11.4.1 Overview

Under the Securities Act of 1933, any **non-exempt** security offered to the public must be **registered with the SEC**, and investors must be given a **prospectus**. The registration process divides into three periods with specific rules about what underwriters may do:

| Period | Begins | What's allowed |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-registration (pre-filing)** | Before the registration statement is filed | **Nothing.** No offers, no sales, no solicitation of interest ("gun-jumping" is prohibited). Normal advertising of the business is fine. |
| **Cooling-off period** | Registration statement filed with the SEC | Lasts at least **20 days** (usually longer). Underwriters may distribute the **preliminary prospectus (red herring)**, publish **tombstone ads**, take **indications of interest**, and hold **road shows / due diligence meetings**. **No sales, no orders, no money accepted, no promises.** The red herring may not be marked up or accompanied by sales literature. |
| **Post-effective (effective) period** | SEC declares the registration **effective** | Sales may be made. Every buyer must receive the **final prospectus** no later than the confirmation of sale. |

### 11.4.2 Preparing for the sale

**Registration statement (Form S-1).** Filed by the issuer with the SEC; its contents become the prospectus. It includes: description of the business, the offering and use of proceeds, risk factors, capitalization, audited financial statements, management and their compensation, major shareholders, legal proceedings, and the underwriting arrangements. The issuer, its directors, officers who sign, auditors, and the underwriters are all **liable** for material misstatements or omissions.

**Preliminary prospectus (red herring).** So called for the red-ink legend on the cover stating that the registration has not yet become effective. Contains everything except the **final public offering price** and the **effective date** (it may show a price range). Used to gauge demand during the cooling-off period.

**Indications of interest.** Non-binding expressions by investors of how many shares they'd like; neither the investor nor the firm is committed. The underwriter uses them to **build the book** and set the price.

**Tombstone advertisement.** A bare-bones ad listing the issuer, the security, the amount, the offering price (or range), and the underwriters — with a statement that it is not an offer to sell and that offers are made only by prospectus. The only advertising permitted during the cooling-off period; it is not deemed an "offer."

**Due diligence meeting.** Held near the end of the cooling-off period among the issuer, underwriters, counsel and accountants to review the registration statement and make sure everything is accurate and complete. Underwriters who perform adequate due diligence have a defense against liability.

**Blue-sky registration.** The offering must also be registered (or qualify for an exemption) in each **state** where it will be sold, under state securities ("blue-sky") laws administered under the Uniform Securities Act. Three methods: **coordination** (filed with the state at the same time as the SEC — typical for IPOs), **qualification** (full state review — used when there's no SEC registration), **notice filing** (for federal covered securities such as exchange-listed stocks and mutual funds — the state just gets a notice and a fee).

**Pricing.** The final price is set on the evening before the effective date based on the book of indications. Underwriters then sign the final underwriting agreement.

### 11.4.3 Effective registration

The SEC reviews the registration statement for completeness and may issue **deficiency letters** (comments) that pause the 20-day clock until fixed. When the SEC is satisfied it declares the registration **effective**. Crucially:

> The SEC does **not** approve, endorse, or pass on the accuracy of a registration statement or the merits of a security. Every prospectus must say so in the **SEC disclaimer**. It is a violation to tell a customer that the SEC "approved" an issue.

**Final prospectus (statutory prospectus).** Contains everything in the red herring plus the final offering price, the underwriting spread, the effective date, and the delivery date. Must be delivered to every purchaser in the offering no later than with the confirmation. The **prospectus delivery requirement** continues into the aftermarket for dealers:

| Offering type | Dealers must deliver a prospectus on aftermarket trades for |
|---|---|
| IPO of a security that will **not** be listed on an exchange/Nasdaq | **90 days** after the effective date |
| IPO of a security that **will** be listed | **25 days** |
| Follow-on offering, non-listed | **40 days** |
| Follow-on offering, listed | 0 days (no aftermarket requirement) |

Access-equals-delivery: posting the prospectus on EDGAR generally satisfies the requirement for IPOs.

**Securities Act liability.** Anyone who buys in a registered offering and suffers a loss because of a material misstatement or omission in the registration statement can sue the issuer, directors, signing officers, experts (auditors) and underwriters (Section 11). Section 12 and Section 17 impose anti-fraud liability for any offer or sale.

**FINRA Rule 5130 — new issue (IPO) allocations.** FINRA member firms may **not sell new issues of common stock to "restricted persons"**:

- FINRA members and their **employees** (associated persons) and their **immediate family** (spouse, children, parents, siblings, in-laws, and anyone they materially support).
- Finders, fiduciaries of the managing underwriter (attorneys, accountants), and portfolio managers who buy for institutions (when buying for their own accounts).
- Any person owning a broker-dealer.

Before selling an IPO to an account, the firm must obtain a written (or electronic) representation from the beneficial owner within the prior **12 months** confirming the account is eligible. Exceptions include accounts with ≤ 10% restricted-person ownership (de minimis), investment companies, and certain issuer-directed shares. The rule aims to keep IPOs — especially "hot issues" that trade at an immediate premium — from going to insiders. **Spinning** (allocating hot IPO shares to executives of companies to win their investment banking business) is separately prohibited by **FINRA Rule 5131**, which also restricts **flipping** penalties and quid pro quo allocations.

### 11.4.4 Exemptions

Not every security or transaction must be registered. Memorize both lists.

**Exempt securities** (never need SEC registration, though anti-fraud rules always apply):

- **US government and federal agency** securities.
- **Municipal** securities.
- Securities issued by **banks** (not bank holding companies) and savings institutions.
- **Commercial paper and other short-term debt with maturities ≤ 270 days**.
- Securities of **non-profit** (religious, educational, charitable) organizations.
- **Insurance policies and fixed annuities** (variable annuities are **not** exempt — they are securities).
- Securities of common carriers (railroads) and small business investment companies.

**Exempt transactions** (the security itself isn't exempt, but the way it is sold is):

| Exemption | Key terms |
|---|---|
| **Regulation D — private placement** | Sold privately, not through general public solicitation (though Rule 506(c) allows advertising if *all* buyers are verified accredited investors). **Rule 506(b)**: unlimited **accredited investors** plus up to **35 non-accredited** (but sophisticated) investors; unlimited dollar amount. **Rule 504**: up to $10 million in 12 months. Buyers receive a **private placement memorandum (PPM)** rather than a prospectus. Securities are **restricted** (must be held, typically 6 months–1 year, before resale under Rule 144). Issuer files **Form D** with the SEC within 15 days of the first sale. |
| **Accredited investor** (the Reg D definition) | Individual with **net worth > $1 million** (excluding primary residence) **or** income > **$200,000** (> **$300,000** with spouse) in each of the last two years with a reasonable expectation of the same; also institutions, banks, insurance companies, registered investment companies, employee benefit plans > $5 million, entities with > $5 million assets, directors/executives of the issuer, and holders of certain securities licenses (Series 7, 65, 82). |
| **Regulation A (Reg A+)** | Small/medium offerings: **Tier 1 up to $20 million**, **Tier 2 up to $75 million** in 12 months. Uses an **offering circular** instead of a prospectus; abbreviated "mini-registration" with the SEC. Tier 2 investors who aren't accredited are limited to 10% of income/net worth. |
| **Rule 147 / 147A — intrastate offering** | Issuer organized/doing business in one state and sells **only to residents of that state**. Purchasers may not resell to non-residents for **6 months**. Still must comply with the state's blue-sky law. |
| **Rule 144A** | Allows resale of **restricted/unregistered securities to qualified institutional buyers (QIBs — institutions with ≥ $100 million in securities)** without registration. Creates a liquid institutional market for private placements. |
| **Regulation S** | Offerings made **outside the US** to non-US persons are exempt from registration. |
| **Regulation Crowdfunding** | Up to **$5 million** per year through an SEC-registered funding portal; individual investment limits based on income/net worth. |
| **Rule 145** | Securities issued in mergers/reclassifications generally require registration; stock splits and dividends do not. |

Being exempt from registration does **not** exempt anyone from the **anti-fraud** provisions. All offers and sales must be truthful.

## 11.5 Rule 144

**Rule 144** governs the resale of **restricted** and **control** securities so that insiders and private-placement buyers can't dump unregistered stock on the public in a way that circumvents the 1933 Act.

**Definitions.**

- **Restricted securities** — unregistered shares acquired from the issuer or an affiliate in a private transaction (Reg D placements, employee stock grants, shares received in a merger). They bear a restrictive legend.
- **Control securities** — shares (registered or not) owned by an **affiliate (control person)** of the issuer — officers, directors, or holders of **10% or more** of the voting stock, and their immediate family.

**Requirements for a sale under Rule 144.**

| Requirement | Restricted securities (non-affiliate) | Control securities (affiliate) |
|---|---|---|
| **Holding period** | **6 months** (if the issuer is an SEC-reporting company; 1 year otherwise), measured from the date fully paid for | No holding period for registered shares; restricted shares held by affiliates still need 6 months |
| **Volume limit** (per 90 days) | None after the holding period (and after 1 year, no other conditions) | Greater of **1% of the outstanding shares** or the **average weekly trading volume over the preceding 4 weeks** |
| **Form 144 filing** | Not required | **Filed with the SEC at or before the sale**; valid for **90 days**; not required if selling ≤ 5,000 shares **and** ≤ $50,000 |
| **Manner of sale** | — | Through a broker (agency) or market maker in ordinary brokerage transactions; no solicitation |
| **Current public information** | Issuer must be current in SEC filings | Same |

**Example (volume limit).** A director wants to sell shares in a company with 20 million shares outstanding. 1% = 200,000. The last four weeks' trading volumes were 150,000 / 200,000 / 250,000 / 300,000 (average 225,000). She may sell up to **225,000 shares** in the next 90 days (the greater of the two).

**Related rules.** Affiliates are also subject to **Section 16** of the 1934 Act — must report holdings and trades (Forms 3, 4, 5) and return **short-swing profits** (buy and sell within 6 months) to the company; insiders may not sell short their own company's stock. **Rule 144A** (above) is the institutional resale route.

## 11.6 Other rules

**Securities Act of 1933 anti-fraud (Section 17).** Unlawful to use any device, scheme or untrue statement in the offer or sale of **any** security — exempt or not.

**Prospectus rules for mutual funds.** Because a mutual fund is a continuous offering, the prospectus must be delivered to every purchaser, must be updated at least every **16 months**, and sales literature may not go beyond it.

**Restrictions on underwriters' compensation (FINRA Rule 5110, the Corporate Financing Rule).** FINRA reviews underwriting terms for fairness; compensation must not be unreasonable; underwriters may not receive certain long-term options/warrants from the issuer.

**Conflicts of interest (FINRA Rule 5121).** If a member underwrites its own securities or those of an affiliate, or has a conflict (e.g., receiving more than 5% of proceeds to repay debt to itself), a **qualified independent underwriter** must participate and the conflict must be prominently disclosed.

**Free-riding and withholding.** Underwriters must make a bona fide public offering at the POP; they may not hold back shares of a hot issue for themselves (this is the origin of Rule 5130).

**Communications during registration.** Rule 134 permits tombstone ads; Rule 135 permits limited announcements of a planned offering; **Rule 163** lets **well-known seasoned issuers (WKSIs)** make offers before filing; **free-writing prospectuses** (Rule 433) are allowed for many issuers once the registration is filed, provided they are filed with the SEC. **Emerging growth companies (EGCs)** under the JOBS Act (< $1.235 billion revenue) may "test the waters" with QIBs and institutions before filing and have reduced disclosure requirements.

**Regulation M.** Prohibits underwriters and issuers from bidding for or buying the security being distributed (except stabilization) during the restricted period, to prevent manipulation.

**Penny stock rules (Rule 15g-9).** Before a broker-dealer sells an unlisted stock priced under $5 to a new customer, it must obtain a **written suitability statement** and the customer's signed agreement for the specific transaction, and provide the risk disclosure document. Established customers (account open > 1 year or 3+ prior penny stock purchases) are exempt from the suitability-statement requirement but not from disclosure. Monthly statements must show the market value of penny stock positions.

**Direct participation program and REIT offerings.** Public DPPs and non-traded REITs are registered offerings but FINRA limits underwriting compensation (10% of gross proceeds) and requires specific suitability standards and disclosures (valuations on statements, etc.).

## Chapter 11 quick review

- Primary market = issuer sells, issuer gets proceeds; Securities Act of 1933; SEC reviews for **completeness, never approves**.
- Spread = manager's fee + underwriting fee + selling concession (largest).
- **Firm commitment** (principal, underwriter bears risk; standby for rights offerings) vs. **best efforts** (agent, issuer bears risk; AON and mini-max use escrow).
- Stabilizing bid: at or below POP, disclosed. Green shoe: +15%.
- Primary offering = new shares, issuer paid. Secondary offering = existing holders sell. Shelf registration: 3 years.
- Pre-filing: nothing. **Cooling-off (≥ 20 days)**: red herring, tombstones, indications of interest, road shows, due diligence — **no sales**. Effective: sell with final prospectus.
- Aftermarket prospectus delivery: 90 days (non-listed IPO), 25 days (listed IPO), 40 days (non-listed follow-on).
- Blue-sky: coordination, qualification, notice filing.
- Exempt securities: governments, munis, banks, ≤ 270-day CP, non-profits, insurance/fixed annuities. Exempt transactions: Reg D (506(b): unlimited accredited + 35 non-accredited; accredited = $1M net worth ex-home or $200k/$300k income), Reg A ($20M/$75M), Rule 147 (intrastate, 6-month resale restriction), 144A (QIBs, $100M), Reg S (offshore), crowdfunding ($5M).
- **Rule 144:** restricted stock 6-month holding period; affiliates limited to the greater of 1% of outstanding or 4-week average weekly volume per 90 days; Form 144 valid 90 days; not needed for ≤ 5,000 shares and ≤ $50,000.
- **Rule 5130:** no IPO shares to FINRA members, their employees and immediate family (restricted persons). **Rule 5131:** no spinning.
- Penny stock: < $5, unlisted; suitability statement + signed agreement for new customers.
