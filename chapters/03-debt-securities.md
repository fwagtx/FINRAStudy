# Chapter 3: Debt Securities (Bond Fundamentals)

> This chapter is the foundation for Chapters 4–6 (corporate, municipal and government debt). Bonds are heavily tested. Master price/yield relationships, the yield types, and bond features before moving on.

---

## 3.1 Basic characteristics

**A bond is a loan.** The investor (bondholder) lends money to the issuer (corporation, municipality or government). The issuer promises to pay **interest** periodically and to return the **principal** at **maturity**. Bondholders are **creditors**, not owners — no vote, no ownership, but priority over all stockholders in liquidation.

**Key terms.**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Par (face) value** | Amount repaid at maturity. Standard **$1,000** per bond. |
| **Coupon (nominal/stated rate)** | Annual interest as a % of par. A 5% coupon pays $50/year, usually **$25 semiannually**. Fixed for the life of the bond. |
| **Maturity date** | When principal is repaid and interest stops. |
| **Indenture** | The legal contract between issuer and bondholders (via a trustee) spelling out the terms, covenants, collateral, call features. Required for corporate bonds over $50 million by the **Trust Indenture Act of 1939**. |
| **Trustee** | Bank/trust company that enforces the indenture on behalf of bondholders. |

**Maturity structures.**

- **Term bond** — entire issue matures on one date. Quoted on price (% of par).
- **Serial bond** — portions of the issue mature in successive years (common for municipals). Quoted on yield (basis).
- **Balloon** — serial with a large final payment.

**Bond pricing conventions.** Bonds are quoted as a **percentage of par** in points. 1 point = $10 (1% of $1,000).

- Quote of **98** = $980 (discount). Quote of **102** = $1,020 (premium). Quote of **100** = par.
- Corporate and municipal bonds are quoted in **1/8ths** of a point: 98 ⅛ = $981.25.
- Treasury notes and bonds are quoted in **1/32nds**: 98.16 (or 98-16) = 98 16/32 = 98.5% = $985.00.

**Price and interest rates move inversely.** This is the single most important bond concept. When market interest rates **rise**, existing bond prices **fall** (their fixed coupons are less attractive). When rates **fall**, bond prices **rise**.

- Longer maturity → **more** price volatility for a given rate change.
- Lower coupon → **more** price volatility. (Zero-coupon bonds are the most volatile.)
- **Duration** measures this sensitivity; higher duration = more interest-rate risk.

**Interest accrual.** Bond interest accrues daily. When a bond is sold between coupon dates, the buyer pays the seller the **accrued interest** since the last payment on top of the price, and then receives the full next coupon.

- Corporate and municipal bonds: **30/360** day count (every month treated as 30 days, year as 360).
- US government bonds: **actual/365** (actual days elapsed).
- Accrued interest runs from the last coupon date **up to but not including the settlement date**.
- Bonds trading without accrued interest (e.g., in default or zero-coupon) trade **flat**.

**Risks of bonds.**

| Risk | Meaning | Most exposed |
|---|---|---|
| Interest-rate risk | Prices fall when rates rise | Long-term, low-coupon bonds |
| Credit (default) risk | Issuer can't pay | Low-rated ("junk") bonds |
| Inflation (purchasing-power) risk | Fixed payments buy less | Long-term bonds |
| Reinvestment risk | Coupons/called principal reinvested at lower rates | Callable, high-coupon bonds; **zero-coupon bonds have none** |
| Call risk | Issuer redeems early when rates fall | Callable bonds |
| Liquidity risk | Hard to sell at fair value | Thinly traded issues |
| Legislative/political risk | Tax law or regulation changes | Municipals |
| Currency risk | Exchange rates | Foreign bonds |

**Credit ratings.** Standard & Poor's, Moody's and Fitch rate issuers' ability to pay.

| Grade | S&P / Fitch | Moody's |
|---|---|---|
| Investment grade (top four tiers) | AAA, AA, A, BBB | Aaa, Aa, A, Baa |
| Speculative / high-yield / "junk" | BB, B, CCC, CC, C | Ba, B, Caa, Ca, C |
| In default | D | C |

**BBB/Baa is the lowest investment-grade rating.** Lower rating → higher yield required (more credit risk). Ratings are the single best measure of a bond's default risk.

## 3.2 Issuance & underwriting

Bonds are issued in the **primary market** through underwriters (investment banks), much like stocks (details in Chapter 11). A few bond-specific points:

- Corporate bonds registered under the Securities Act of 1933 need a prospectus; many corporate debt offerings are sold to institutions as **private placements** or under Rule 144A.
- Municipal bonds are **exempt** from SEC registration; instead, the issuer prepares an **official statement**.
- US government securities are sold at **Treasury auctions**.
- **Competitive bid** underwriting (common for general obligation munis) vs. **negotiated** underwriting (common for corporates and revenue bonds).
- A bond sold at issue for exactly par is "priced at par"; new issues are often priced near par with the coupon set to current market yields.
- Bonds come in **registered form** (owner recorded by the issuer/transfer agent — this is standard now) or **book-entry** (electronic, no certificate — Treasuries and most new issues). **Bearer bonds** (no owner record, coupons physically clipped) are no longer issued in the US (prohibited since 1982) but older ones may still exist.

## 3.3 Trading

Most bonds trade **over-the-counter (OTC)** in a dealer market rather than on an exchange. Dealers quote a **bid** (what they'll pay) and an **ask/offer** (what they'll sell for); the difference is the **spread**.

- **Markup/markdown** — when a dealer trades with a customer from its own inventory (principal), it charges a markup on sales or markdown on purchases. Must be fair and reasonable; FINRA's **5% policy** is a guideline (not a rule) for fairness in secondary trades.
- Corporate bond trades are reported to FINRA's **TRACE** (Trade Reporting and Compliance Engine) within 15 minutes. Municipal trades are reported to **MSRB's EMMA/RTRS** system.
- **Settlement: T+1** for corporate and municipal bonds (regular way). Treasuries also T+1.
- Bond quotes: term bonds on **price** (dollar quotes), serial bonds on **yield (basis)**.
- Bonds trading at a discount are "discount bonds," at a premium "premium bonds."

**Basis points.** 1 basis point (bp) = 0.01%. 100 bp = 1%. A yield moving from 4.25% to 4.75% rose 50 bp.

## 3.4 Features

### Callable bonds

The issuer may redeem the bond before maturity at a stated **call price** (par or a premium), after a **call protection** period. Issuers call when rates **fall** to refinance cheaply (a "refunding"). Investors face call risk and reinvestment risk, so callable bonds pay **higher** coupons. Calls can be:

- **Whole call** — the entire issue.
- **Partial call** — a portion, often selected by lot.
- **Sinking fund** — the issuer sets aside money regularly to retire bonds over time (reduces default risk).
- **Pre-refunding / advance refunding** — issuer sells a new bond and puts the proceeds in escrow (Treasuries) to pay off the old one on its call date; the old bond becomes very safe ("escrowed to maturity").

### Puttable bonds

The **investor** may force the issuer to buy the bond back at par on specified dates. Investor-friendly → **lower** coupon. Investors exercise when rates **rise** (to reinvest at higher rates).

### Convertible bonds

Can be exchanged for a fixed number of the issuer's common shares. **Conversion ratio** = par ÷ conversion price. A bond convertible at $40 converts to $1,000 / $40 = **25 shares**. **Parity price** of the bond = stock price × conversion ratio; parity of the stock = bond price ÷ ratio. Lower coupon (investor-friendly); price tracks the stock when it's near/above parity. An **anti-dilution** provision adjusts the conversion price for stock splits/dividends. Covered more in Chapter 4.

### Zero-coupon bonds

Pay no periodic interest; issued at a **deep discount** and mature at par. The return is the difference. Examples: Treasury STRIPS, corporate and municipal zeros.

- **No reinvestment risk** (no coupons to reinvest) — ideal for a known future goal (college, retirement).
- **Most interest-rate-sensitive** (highest duration) for a given maturity.
- The annual accretion of the discount is **taxable each year** as interest even though no cash is received ("phantom income") — except for municipal zeros, which are tax-exempt. Good candidates for tax-deferred accounts.

### Secured vs. unsecured (covered in detail in Chapter 4)

Secured bonds are backed by specific collateral (mortgage bonds, equipment trust certificates, collateral trust bonds). Unsecured bonds (**debentures**) are backed only by the issuer's full faith and credit. Subordinated debentures rank below other debentures.

### Variable-rate (floating-rate) bonds

Coupon resets periodically to a benchmark. Price stays near par; minimal interest-rate risk.

### Step-coupon / step-up bonds

Coupon increases on a set schedule.

## 3.5 Yield

A bond's **yield** is its return expressed as an annual percentage. There are several, and the exam tests how they relate.

### Nominal yield (coupon rate)

The stated rate. Fixed. 6% coupon → $60/year regardless of price.

### Current yield (CY)

```
Current yield = Annual interest ÷ Current market price
```

A 6% bond trading at $900: CY = $60 / $900 = 6.67%. At $1,100: $60 / $1,100 = 5.45%.

### Yield to maturity (YTM, "basis")

The total annualized return if held to maturity — includes the coupons **and** the gain (discount bond) or loss (premium bond) of principal at maturity. A bond bought at a discount has YTM > CY > nominal. A bond bought at a premium has YTM < CY < nominal. Serial bonds are quoted on YTM ("5.10 basis").

Approximate formula (for understanding, rarely required on the SIE):

```
YTM ≈ [Annual interest + (Par − Price) ÷ Years to maturity] ÷ [(Par + Price) ÷ 2]
```

### Yield to call (YTC)

Like YTM but assumes the bond is called at the first call date and call price. For a **premium bond, YTC is the lowest yield** (you lose the premium faster). For a discount bond, YTC is higher than YTM.

### Yield to worst

The lowest of all possible yields (YTM vs. each YTC). Dealers must quote the **lowest** yield (yield to worst) on bond confirmations for callable bonds.

### The relationships (memorize)

| Bond trading at | Order of yields |
|---|---|
| **Discount** (price < par) | Nominal < Current yield < YTM < YTC |
| **Par** | All yields equal |
| **Premium** (price > par) | Nominal > Current yield > YTM > YTC |

Mental model: a discount bond gives you a bonus at maturity, so its "all-in" yields exceed the coupon; a premium bond makes you give some back, so they're lower.

**Rates up → prices down → yields up.** Price and yield move inversely; price and interest rates move inversely; yield and interest rates move together.

### The yield curve

A plot of yields vs. maturity for similar-credit bonds (usually Treasuries).

- **Normal (positive/upward sloping)** — long-term yields > short-term. The typical shape; investors demand more for lending longer.
- **Flat** — short and long roughly equal; often precedes a slowdown.
- **Inverted (negative)** — short-term yields > long-term. Historically a recession signal; usually results from the Fed tightening.

**Credit spread** — the yield difference between corporate bonds and Treasuries of the same maturity. Spreads **widen** when investors fear defaults (recession) and **narrow** in good times.

## 3.6 Suitability

Bonds suit investors seeking **income** and/or **preservation of capital**, with lower risk than stocks. Match the bond to the investor:

| Investor need | Appropriate choice |
|---|---|
| Maximum safety | US Treasuries, then agencies, then investment-grade |
| Tax-free income (high bracket) | Municipal bonds (Chapter 5) |
| Income in a tax-deferred account | Corporate bonds (higher yield; taxes don't matter inside the account) |
| Known future lump sum, no reinvestment risk | Zero-coupon bonds |
| Expecting rates to rise | Short-term bonds, floating-rate bonds, puttable bonds |
| Expecting rates to fall | Long-term, non-callable bonds (lock in high yield, maximum price gain) |
| Higher income, can tolerate default risk | High-yield (junk) bonds |
| Income plus equity upside | Convertible bonds |
| Concerned about inflation | TIPS (Chapter 6), short maturities |

**Bond laddering** — buying bonds with staggered maturities to reduce interest-rate and reinvestment risk. **Barbell** — concentrate in short and long maturities. **Bullet** — all mature around one date.

## Chapter 3 quick review

- Par $1,000; coupon fixed, paid semiannually; bondholders are creditors.
- Price quoted as % of par; 1 point = $10; corporates in 1/8ths, Treasuries in 1/32nds.
- **Rates up, prices down.** Long maturity and low coupon = most volatile.
- Accrued interest: corporate/muni 30/360; government actual/365; up to but not including settlement.
- Ratings: BBB/Baa is the lowest investment grade; below is junk.
- Callable → higher coupon, called when rates fall. Puttable → lower coupon, put when rates rise.
- Zero coupon: deep discount, no reinvestment risk, most rate-sensitive, phantom income taxed annually (unless muni).
- Yields: discount bond → Nominal < CY < YTM < YTC; premium bond → the reverse. Quote yield to worst.
- Yield curve: normal, flat, inverted (recession signal). Credit spreads widen in bad times.
- Settlement T+1; TRACE for corporates; MSRB for munis.
