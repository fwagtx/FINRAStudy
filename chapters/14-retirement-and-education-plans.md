# Chapter 14: Retirement & Education Plans

> IRAs, employer plans, ERISA, variable annuities and variable life, 529s and Coverdells. Expect questions on traditional vs. Roth, contribution rules, early-withdrawal penalties, RMDs, annuity phases and death benefits, and the tax treatment of each plan. Dollar limits shown are **2026** figures (the exam emphasizes rules over exact amounts).

---

## 14.1 Generalities

**Why retirement plans exist.** Tax law encourages saving for retirement by giving plans one of two tax advantages:

- **Tax-deferred (pre-tax)** — contributions are deductible/excluded from income now, earnings grow untaxed, and **all withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income** later (traditional IRA, 401(k), 403(b), SEP, SIMPLE, non-qualified annuity *earnings*).
- **Tax-free (after-tax)** — contributions are made with taxed dollars, earnings grow untaxed, and **qualified withdrawals are entirely tax-free** (Roth IRA, Roth 401(k), 529 plans, Coverdell ESAs, HSAs).

Either way, growth inside the plan is **not taxed while it compounds** — the central benefit.

**Qualified vs. non-qualified plans.**

| | **Qualified plan** | **Non-qualified plan** |
|---|---|---|
| Meets IRS/ERISA requirements | Yes | No |
| Employer contributions deductible | Yes, immediately | Only when the employee is taxed |
| Must be offered to all eligible employees (non-discrimination) | **Yes** | No — can favor executives |
| Contributions | Pre-tax | After-tax |
| Growth | Tax-deferred | Tax-deferred |
| Withdrawals | Fully taxable (ordinary income) | Only **earnings** taxable |
| Examples | 401(k), 403(b), defined benefit pensions, profit-sharing, Keogh/HR-10 | Deferred compensation plans, payroll-deduction plans, non-qualified annuities |

IRAs are not "qualified plans" in the ERISA sense, but they share the tax-deferral features.

**Suitability basics.** Tax-advantaged accounts should hold investments that benefit from deferral — growth stocks, taxable bonds, REITs, zero-coupon bonds. They should **not** hold **municipal bonds** (tax-free yield wasted), and **annuities** inside an IRA add cost without added tax benefit (acceptable only for the annuity's guarantees). Margin is not allowed in retirement accounts; option strategies are limited to the conservative ones.

## 14.2 Rules

### ERISA

The **Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974** governs **private-sector** employer retirement plans (not government or church plans, not IRAs). Its purpose: protect participants from mismanagement and abuse. Key requirements:

- **Participation (eligibility)** — employees who are at least **21** and have **one year of service** (1,000 hours) must be allowed to participate (long-term part-time employees with 500 hours for 2 years now also qualify).
- **Funding** — contributions must be segregated from employer assets (held in trust).
- **Vesting** — the employee's right to **employer** contributions becomes non-forfeitable on a schedule (cliff vesting by 3 years or graded by 6 years for DC plans). **Employee contributions are always 100% vested immediately.**
- **Non-discrimination** — the plan can't favor highly compensated employees; tested annually.
- **Fiduciary responsibility** — those who manage the plan must act **solely in the interest of participants** (the **prudent expert rule**), diversify, and follow the plan documents. An **investment policy statement** guides the plan's investments.
- **Reporting and disclosure** — participants get a summary plan description, annual statements; plans file Form 5500.
- **Beneficiary rules** — a married participant's spouse is the default beneficiary unless the spouse waives in writing.

### Distributions and penalties (apply to IRAs and most qualified plans)

- Withdrawals of pre-tax money are taxed as **ordinary income** — never as capital gains, regardless of what was earned inside.
- **Early withdrawal penalty:** **10%** additional tax on distributions taken **before age 59½**, on top of income tax. Exceptions (no penalty, tax still due): **death**, **disability**, substantially equal periodic payments (**Rule 72(t)**), medical expenses above a threshold, qualified **first-time home purchase (up to $10,000 lifetime — IRAs)**, qualified **higher education expenses (IRAs)**, health insurance while unemployed (IRAs), birth/adoption (up to $5,000), **separation from service at age 55+** (employer plans only), qualified domestic relations orders (QDROs), terminal illness, emergency withdrawals up to $1,000/year, and certain disaster distributions.
- **Required minimum distributions (RMDs):** must begin by **April 1 of the year after turning 73** (age 75 for those born in 1960 or later), then by December 31 each year. Based on the account balance divided by an IRS life-expectancy factor. **Roth IRAs have no lifetime RMDs** for the owner (and Roth 401(k)s no longer require them either). Penalty for missing an RMD: **25%** of the shortfall (reduced to 10% if corrected promptly). Still-working employees can often delay RMDs from their current employer's plan.
- **Rollovers and transfers.** A **direct transfer (trustee-to-trustee)** moves money between custodians without the owner touching it — unlimited, no tax. A **rollover** is when the owner receives a check and must redeposit it within **60 days** — allowed **once per 12 months** for IRA-to-IRA rollovers; distributions from employer plans paid to the participant are subject to **20% mandatory withholding** (which the participant must make up from other funds to roll over the full amount). A direct rollover from an employer plan to an IRA avoids withholding.
- **Beneficiaries.** Spouses can treat an inherited IRA as their own. Most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited IRA within **10 years** (SECURE Act). Assets in retirement accounts pass by **beneficiary designation**, not by will.
- **Excess contributions** are subject to a **6% penalty** each year until removed.

## 14.3 Workplace plans

### Defined benefit vs. defined contribution

| | **Defined benefit (DB) — traditional pension** | **Defined contribution (DC)** |
|---|---|---|
| What is defined | The **benefit** at retirement (e.g., 2% × years of service × final salary) | The **contribution** going in (e.g., 5% of salary + employer match) |
| Investment risk borne by | **Employer** (must fund the promised benefit; actuaries determine contributions) | **Employee** (benefit depends on investment results) |
| Favors | **Older, highly paid** employees with few years to retirement (larger contributions needed to fund the benefit) | **Younger** employees (longer time to compound) |
| Examples | Corporate and government pensions | 401(k), 403(b), profit-sharing, money purchase, ESOP, SIMPLE, SEP |
| Insured by | **PBGC** (Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation) | Not insured |

### 401(k) plans

The dominant private-sector DC plan. Employees defer part of salary **pre-tax** (traditional) or **after-tax (Roth 401(k))** via payroll deduction; employers often **match** (e.g., 50% of the first 6%). 2026 employee deferral limit: **$24,500**; catch-up for age 50+: **$8,000** (super catch-up at ages 60–63: **$11,250**); total annual additions (employee + employer) limit: **$72,000**. Loans are often permitted (up to 50% of vested balance or $50,000). Employer contributions vest on a schedule. Highly compensated employees are subject to non-discrimination testing unless the plan is a "safe harbor" plan. **Automatic enrollment** is now required for many new plans. Hardship withdrawals are allowed but taxed and penalized.

- **Solo (individual) 401(k)** — for self-employed persons with no employees.
- **Roth 401(k)** — same limits, after-tax contributions, tax-free qualified withdrawals; no income limits (unlike Roth IRAs).

### 403(b) plans (tax-sheltered annuities, TSAs)

For employees of **public schools, colleges, hospitals, and 501(c)(3) non-profit organizations** (churches, charities). Same contribution limits as 401(k)s. Investments are limited to **annuity contracts and mutual funds** (no individual stocks). Contributions are pre-tax (or Roth); withdrawals taxable; 10% penalty before 59½. Long-tenured employees may have an extra catch-up.

### 457 plans

Deferred compensation plans for **state and local government employees** and some non-profits. Same deferral limits; **no 10% early withdrawal penalty** on separation from service (unique advantage); can be contributed to **in addition to** a 401(k)/403(b).

### Profit-sharing plans

Employer contributions are **discretionary** (tied to profits or not) — flexible for the employer; no required annual contribution. Allocated to employees by a formula; same annual addition limits.

### Money purchase plans

Employer must contribute a **fixed percentage** of each employee's salary every year, profit or not.

### Employee stock ownership plans (ESOPs) and stock bonus plans

The plan invests primarily in the **employer's stock**; participants receive shares. Concentration risk — the exam notes the lack of diversification.

### SEP IRAs (Simplified Employee Pension)

For **small businesses and self-employed**. **Only the employer contributes** (up to 25% of compensation, max **$72,000** in 2026), and the same percentage must go to all eligible employees (age 21, worked 3 of the last 5 years, earned $800+). Contributions are immediately 100% vested, held in each employee's own IRA. Easy to set up; no annual filing.

### SIMPLE IRAs / SIMPLE 401(k)s (Savings Incentive Match Plan for Employees)

For employers with **100 or fewer employees** that have **no other retirement plan**. Employees defer up to **$17,000** (2026; catch-up $4,000 at 50+, $5,250 at 60–63); the employer must either **match up to 3%** of pay or contribute **2%** for everyone. Immediately vested. Early withdrawal within the **first 2 years** carries a **25%** penalty (instead of 10%).

### Keogh (HR-10) plans

Older term for qualified plans of **self-employed** individuals and unincorporated businesses (sole proprietors, partnerships); contribution based on self-employment income. Largely replaced by SEPs and solo 401(k)s.

### Non-qualified deferred compensation

An unsecured promise by the employer to pay an executive later; the employee is a **general creditor** if the firm fails; can be discriminatory; no immediate deduction for the employer. **Payroll deduction plans** (after-tax savings taken from paychecks) are also non-qualified.

## 14.4 Individual retirement accounts (IRAs)

Anyone with **earned income** (wages, salary, self-employment income, alimony under pre-2019 agreements — **not** interest, dividends, capital gains, pension, Social Security or rental income) may contribute to an IRA. A **spousal IRA** lets a non-working spouse contribute based on the working spouse's income if filing jointly. There is no longer an age limit for traditional IRA contributions.

**Contribution limits (2026):** **$7,500** per year, plus **$1,100** catch-up for age **50+**, or 100% of earned income if less. The limit is **combined** across all traditional and Roth IRAs. Contributions for a tax year may be made up to the **tax-filing deadline (April 15)** of the following year, no extensions. Excess contributions are penalized **6%** per year.

### Traditional IRA

- Contributions are **tax-deductible** unless the individual (or spouse) is **covered by an employer plan** *and* income exceeds phase-out thresholds — in which case the contribution may still be made but is **non-deductible** (creating after-tax basis in the IRA).
- Earnings grow **tax-deferred**.
- Withdrawals of deductible contributions and all earnings are taxed as **ordinary income**; non-deductible contributions come out tax-free (pro-rata).
- **10% penalty** before **59½** (with the exceptions listed in 14.2).
- **RMDs** required starting at **73** (75 for those born 1960+).

### Roth IRA

- Contributions are **never deductible** (after-tax money).
- **Qualified withdrawals are completely tax-free**: the account must be open **5 years** (from the first contribution) **and** the owner must be **59½** (or dead, disabled, or using up to $10,000 for a first home). Contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn at any time tax- and penalty-free because they were already taxed.
- **No RMDs** during the owner's lifetime.
- **Income limits** apply: high earners phase out of eligibility to contribute directly (a "backdoor" conversion route exists).
- **Roth conversion:** a traditional IRA may be converted to a Roth at any income level; the converted pre-tax amount is **taxed as ordinary income** in the year of conversion (no 10% penalty).
- Best for people who expect to be in a **higher** bracket in retirement, young savers, and those who want to leave tax-free money to heirs.

| | Traditional IRA | Roth IRA |
|---|---|---|
| Contribution | Pre-tax (deductible, subject to limits) | After-tax |
| Growth | Tax-deferred | Tax-free |
| Qualified withdrawals | Taxed as ordinary income | Tax-free |
| Early withdrawal | Tax + 10% penalty on everything | Contributions free anytime; earnings taxed + 10% if non-qualified |
| RMDs | Yes, at 73/75 | No (owner) |
| Income limit to contribute | None (limit on deductibility) | Yes |

### IRA investment rules

- **Permitted:** stocks, bonds, mutual funds, ETFs, CDs, annuities, US government gold/silver coins and certain bullion, REITs, options strategies that are not speculative (covered calls, protective puts).
- **Prohibited/ineligible:** **collectibles** (art, antiques, stamps, most coins, gems, wine), **life insurance**, **margin trading**, short sales, uncovered options, and **municipal bonds** are not prohibited but are **unsuitable**. **Prohibited transactions** (self-dealing — borrowing from the IRA, pledging it as collateral, buying property for personal use) disqualify the entire IRA.
- The IRA must be held by a **custodian/trustee** (bank, broker-dealer, trust company).

### Other IRA-type accounts

- **SEP IRA, SIMPLE IRA** — employer-sponsored (14.3).
- **Inherited (beneficiary) IRA** — 10-year rule for most non-spouse beneficiaries.
- **Rollover IRA** — receives money from an employer plan; keeps the option of rolling back into a future employer plan.
- **Health savings account (HSA)** — triple tax advantage (deductible, tax-free growth, tax-free for medical); requires a high-deductible health plan; 2026 limits $4,400 individual / $8,750 family.

## 14.5 Variable life insurance

**Life insurance basics.** The policy owner pays **premiums**; the insurer pays a **death benefit** to the beneficiary when the insured dies. Death benefits are generally **income-tax-free** to the beneficiary.

- **Term life** — pure protection for a set period; no cash value; cheapest; not a security.
- **Whole life** — permanent coverage with a guaranteed cash value growing at a fixed rate; fixed premium; the insurer bears investment risk; not a security.
- **Universal life** — flexible premiums and death benefit; cash value earns a rate the insurer declares; not a security.
- **Variable life (VL)** — fixed premium; cash value and part of the death benefit **vary** with the performance of **separate account** sub-accounts chosen by the owner. **A security** (requires Series 6/7 + state insurance license, prospectus delivery). There is a **guaranteed minimum death benefit** (the face amount) as long as premiums are paid, but **no guaranteed cash value** — the owner bears investment risk.
- **Variable universal life (VUL)** — variable life with flexible premiums; death benefit may fall below the initial face if the cash value is poor; also a security.

**Variable life features the exam tests.**

- Premiums go partly to insurance costs/expenses and partly into the **separate account**, which is registered under the Investment Company Act of 1940 (as a UIT or open-end company); the policy is registered under the 1933 Act. Sales require **both** a securities registration and a **state insurance license**.
- **Cash value** is not guaranteed; it can be borrowed against (loans reduce the death benefit if not repaid) and grows tax-deferred.
- **Death benefit** has a guaranteed floor equal to the face amount; it is recalculated at least **annually** (cash value at least monthly).
- **Contract exchange (free-look) rights:** the owner may exchange a variable life policy for a traditional fixed/whole life policy from the same insurer, without evidence of insurability, for at least **24 months** after issue (federal rule; many states allow longer). All variable policies have a **free-look period** (typically 10 days, up to 45 days for variable life under some rules) to cancel for a full refund.
- **Sales charges** are capped at **9%** of the total premiums over the life of the policy (maximum 20 years); refunds of excess sales charges are required if the policy is surrendered in the first two years.
- Suitability: for people who need **permanent life insurance** *and* want equity-based growth in cash value and can tolerate investment risk; not a pure investment and not for those who need guarantees.
- **1035 exchange** — tax-free exchange of one life insurance policy or annuity for another (life → life, life → annuity, annuity → annuity; **annuity → life is not allowed**).

## 14.6 Variable annuities

An **annuity** is a contract with an **insurance company** that provides tax-deferred growth and, at the owner's option, a stream of payments for life (or a period). Annuities are the insurance industry's answer to outliving one's money (**longevity risk**).

**Fixed annuity** — the insurer guarantees a **fixed rate** and fixed payments; the **insurer** bears the investment risk; the owner bears **inflation (purchasing-power) risk**. Backed by the insurer's **general account**. **Not a security** — only an insurance license is needed.

**Variable annuity (VA)** — the owner directs premiums into **sub-accounts** (mutual-fund-like portfolios) within the insurer's **separate account**; the value and the eventual payments **vary** with investment results; the **owner bears the investment risk**. **A security** — requires a **prospectus**, SEC registration (separate account under the 1940 Act), and reps need a securities registration (Series 6 or 7) plus a state insurance license. Sold by FINRA members; FINRA Rule 2330 requires principal review of VA purchases within 7 business days and specific suitability.

**Indexed (equity-indexed) annuity** — a fixed annuity whose credited rate is tied to an index (S&P 500) with a **floor** (often 0%) and a **cap/participation rate**; generally treated as insurance, not a security, but FINRA has suitability concerns.

**Two phases.**

| Phase | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Accumulation (pay-in) phase** | Owner contributes (lump sum, periodic, or single premium) and buys **accumulation units**; value grows tax-deferred; withdrawals allowed but taxed (**LIFO** — earnings come out first, taxed as ordinary income) and subject to the **10% penalty before 59½** and the insurer's **surrender charges** (typically declining over 5–10 years). |
| **Annuitization (payout) phase** | Accumulation units convert to a fixed number of **annuity units**; the insurer makes payments for life or a period. Each payment's **value varies** with the separate account's performance relative to the **assumed interest rate (AIR)**: if the sub-accounts earn **more than the AIR**, the next payment **rises**; less → it **falls**; exactly the AIR → unchanged. Once annuitized, the decision is irrevocable. |

**Payout options** (settlement options, from highest to lowest monthly payment):

1. **Life (straight life / life only)** — payments for the annuitant's life only; stop at death. **Highest** payment, most risk (die early, lose the balance).
2. **Life with period certain** — for life, but at least a guaranteed number of years (e.g., 10) to a beneficiary.
3. **Unit refund life** — life payments, and any remaining value at death goes to the beneficiary.
4. **Joint and last survivor** — payments continue until the second of two annuitants dies. **Lowest** payment (longest expected period).
5. Period certain only (not life-based) and lump-sum/systematic withdrawals are alternatives to annuitizing.

**Taxation.**

- Purchased with **after-tax** dollars (non-qualified annuity): contributions are the **cost basis** (not taxed again); **earnings are taxed as ordinary income** when withdrawn. Random withdrawals are **LIFO** (earnings first). Annuitized payments use an **exclusion ratio** — part of each payment is a tax-free return of basis, part taxable.
- In a **qualified** plan (IRA, 403(b)), everything comes out taxable.
- **10% penalty** on earnings withdrawn before **59½**.
- **Death:** the beneficiary receives at least the **guaranteed minimum death benefit** (typically the greater of contract value or total premiums paid, less withdrawals) — this is the insurance element. Gains are taxable to the beneficiary as ordinary income (no step-up in basis).
- **1035 exchange** — annuity-to-annuity swaps are tax-free, but surrender charges on the old contract and a new surrender period on the new one are a red flag for unsuitable "churning."
- No contribution limits for non-qualified annuities; no RMDs during the owner's life.

**Costs** (why VAs are controversial): **mortality and expense (M&E) risk charge** (pays for the death benefit guarantee and the insurer's expense risk), administrative fees, sub-account management fees, surrender charges, and charges for optional **living benefit riders** (guaranteed minimum income/withdrawal/accumulation benefits — GMIB, GMWB, GMAB). Total costs often run 2–3% a year.

**Suitability.** For investors who have **maxed out** other tax-deferred plans, have a **long time horizon** (to outlast surrender charges), want tax deferral and a lifetime income option or the death benefit, and can bear market risk. **Unsuitable** for: short horizons, seniors who may need liquidity, or placing inside an IRA solely for tax deferral (already tax-deferred). Reps must not recommend exchanges that mainly generate new commissions.

## 14.7 Education & other plans

### 529 plans (qualified tuition programs)

State-sponsored plans under IRC Section 529. Two types: **prepaid tuition plans** (lock in today's tuition at participating in-state public schools) and **college savings plans** (invest in mutual-fund portfolios, often age-based).

- Contributions are **after-tax** for federal purposes (many states give a state deduction/credit); earnings grow **tax-deferred**; withdrawals for **qualified education expenses** (tuition, fees, books, room & board for college; K–12 expenses up to **$20,000 per year per beneficiary from 2026** (was $10,000 of tuition only through 2025; the 2025 tax law also broadened K–12 expenses to include books, tutoring and testing fees); registered apprenticeships; up to $10,000 lifetime of student loan repayment) are **federally tax-free**.
- **No income limits** on contributors; high contribution limits (set by each state, often $300,000–$550,000 aggregate).
- Contributions are completed gifts: up to the annual exclusion (**$19,000** in 2026) per beneficiary, or a special **5-year front-loading** election (**$95,000** at once, $190,000 for a couple) with no gift tax.
- The **account owner (donor) keeps control** — not the beneficiary — and can change the beneficiary to another **family member** without tax. Assets are counted as the parent's for financial aid (favorable).
- **Non-qualified withdrawals:** earnings taxed as ordinary income plus a **10% penalty** (penalty waived for death, disability, scholarship up to the scholarship amount). Unused 529 funds (account open 15+ years) can be rolled to the beneficiary's Roth IRA up to a $35,000 lifetime limit.
- Sold by prospectus-like **official statement/program disclosure**; MSRB rules apply because they are municipal fund securities (reps need a Series 6/7 and must disclose if an out-of-state plan may lose state tax benefits).

### Coverdell Education Savings Accounts (ESAs)

- After-tax contributions up to **$2,000 per beneficiary per year** (all contributors combined), until the beneficiary turns **18**.
- Tax-free growth and withdrawals for **qualified K–12 or college** expenses.
- **Income limits** apply to contributors (phase-outs for high earners).
- Funds must be used by age **30** (or rolled to another family member) or earnings are taxed + 10% penalty.
- Self-directed — can hold any permitted investment, like an IRA.

| | 529 | Coverdell ESA |
|---|---|---|
| Annual contribution | Gift-tax limit ($19,000; 5-yr election) | $2,000 |
| Income limits | None | Yes |
| Investment choice | Plan's menu | Any (self-directed) |
| K–12 | Up to $20,000/yr from 2026 ($10,000 tuition-only before) | All qualified K–12 expenses |
| Age limits | None | Contribute until 18; use by 30 |
| Control | Account owner | Custodian/responsible individual |

### UGMA/UTMA accounts for education

Covered in Chapter 13 — the gift is irrevocable, the money belongs to the minor at majority (and can be spent on anything), and it is counted as the child's asset for financial aid (unfavorable). Taxed under the **kiddie tax** rules.

### ABLE accounts (529A)

Tax-advantaged savings for individuals with **disabilities** that began before age 26 (46 from 2026); contributions limited to the annual gift exclusion; withdrawals tax-free for qualified disability expenses; don't jeopardize SSI/Medicaid benefits up to limits.

### Health savings accounts (HSAs)

Mentioned in 14.4 — the only account that is deductible going in, tax-free growing, and tax-free coming out for medical expenses; after 65 can be used for anything (taxed like an IRA).

### Other plans

- **Series EE/I savings bonds** — interest may be federally tax-free when used for education (income limits).
- **Deferred compensation and non-qualified plans** — 14.3.
- **Social Security** — government pension funded by FICA; benefits based on earnings history; full retirement age 66–67.

## Chapter 14 quick review

- Pre-tax plans (traditional IRA/401(k)): deductible in, ordinary income out. After-tax plans (Roth, 529, Coverdell): taxed in, tax-free out if qualified. Growth untaxed in both.
- Qualified plans: non-discriminatory, employer deduction, ERISA. Non-qualified: can discriminate, after-tax, only earnings taxed.
- ERISA: age 21 + 1 year, vesting (employee money always vested), non-discrimination, fiduciary/prudent expert, spouse default beneficiary; private sector only.
- **10% penalty before 59½** (exceptions: death, disability, 72(t), medical, first home $10k, education, 55+ separation); **RMDs at 73** (75 if born 1960+), 25% penalty; Roth IRA no RMDs. Rollover 60 days, once/12 months, 20% withholding on plan checks; direct transfers unlimited.
- DB: benefit fixed, employer risk, favors older/high earners, PBGC. DC: contribution fixed, employee risk, favors younger.
- 401(k): $24,500 (+$8,000 at 50; $11,250 at 60–63); 403(b): schools/non-profits, annuities & funds only; 457: government, no 10% penalty; SEP: employer-only, 25%/$72,000; SIMPLE: ≤ 100 employees, $17,000, 3% match or 2%, 25% penalty first 2 years.
- IRA: earned income required; $7,500 + $1,100 catch-up; deadline April 15; 6% excess penalty. Traditional: deductible unless covered by plan + high income. Roth: after-tax, tax-free if 5 years + 59½, no RMDs, income limits, conversions taxable. No collectibles, life insurance, margin; munis unsuitable.
- Variable life: fixed premium, guaranteed minimum death benefit, cash value not guaranteed, separate account, security + insurance license, 24-month exchange to fixed policy, 9% sales charge cap.
- Variable annuity: security, separate account, owner bears risk; accumulation units → annuity units; payments change vs. **AIR**; straight life = highest payment, joint & survivor = lowest; LIFO on withdrawals, earnings ordinary income, 10% before 59½, surrender charges, M&E charge, death benefit ≥ premiums; 1035 exchanges tax-free. Fixed annuity: insurer bears risk, inflation risk for owner, not a security.
- **529:** after-tax, federally tax-free for education, no income limit, 5-year gifting ($95,000), owner controls, change beneficiary within family, 10% penalty on non-qualified earnings. **Coverdell:** $2,000/yr, income limits, until 18, use by 30, K–12 OK.
