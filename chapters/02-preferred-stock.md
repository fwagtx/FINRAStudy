# Chapter 2: Preferred Stock

> Preferred stock is an equity security that behaves a lot like a bond. The exam loves to test the *differences* between preferred and common, the features (cumulative, participating, convertible, callable, adjustable-rate), and the interest-rate sensitivity of preferred.

---

## 2.1 Basic characteristics

**What it is.** Preferred stock is an ownership (equity) security with a **fixed dividend** stated either as a dollar amount or a percentage of **par value**. Par for preferred is usually **$100** (sometimes $25 or $50). A "6% preferred" with $100 par pays $6 per year, normally in quarterly installments of $1.50.

**"Preferred" in two ways.** Preferred stockholders have priority over common stockholders:

1. **Dividends** — preferred dividends must be paid in full before common stockholders receive anything.
2. **Liquidation** — preferred is paid after all creditors (bondholders) but before common.

**What preferred does NOT have.**

- Usually **no voting rights** (some issues gain voting rights if dividends are missed for a period).
- **No preemptive rights** in most cases.
- **No maturity date** — like common stock, it is perpetual (unless called).
- **Limited growth potential** — because the dividend is fixed, the price doesn't rise much when the company does well (except convertible preferred).

**Dividends are still not guaranteed.** The board must declare them. But because missed preferred dividends block common dividends, companies strongly prefer to pay them.

**Why investors buy preferred.** Fixed income, higher than typical common dividend yields, with priority over common. Corporations that own preferred stock of other companies get a **dividends-received deduction** (a corporation can exclude a large portion of dividends it receives from other domestic corporations from taxable income), which makes preferred especially attractive to corporate investors.

**Interest-rate sensitivity.** Because the dividend is fixed, preferred stock trades like a long-term bond: when interest rates rise, the price of existing preferred falls (its fixed payment becomes less attractive); when rates fall, the price rises. This is **interest-rate risk**. Preferred also carries **inflation (purchasing-power) risk** because the fixed payment loses value over time.

**Pricing.** Preferred prices move inversely to rates. If a $100-par 6% preferred is issued when comparable yields are 6%, and yields rise to 8%, the price falls toward $75 ($6 ÷ 8%). **Current yield** = annual dividend ÷ market price.

## 2.2 Features

Issuers add features to make preferred stock more attractive to investors (which lowers the dividend they must pay) or more flexible for themselves (which requires a higher dividend). Know each one and whether it benefits the issuer or the investor.

### Straight (non-cumulative) preferred

The plain version. If a dividend is skipped, it is gone forever — the company never has to make it up. Least investor-friendly, so it pays the highest dividend of the basic types.

### Cumulative preferred

If a dividend is missed, it **accumulates** as "dividends in arrears." **All arrears plus the current preferred dividend must be paid before any common dividend.** Investor-friendly → lower stated dividend than straight preferred.

**Example.** A company has a $5 cumulative preferred. It skips dividends for two years, then wants to pay a common dividend in year three. It must first pay preferred holders $5 + $5 (arrears) + $5 (current) = **$15** per share.

If the preferred is *non-cumulative*, it would only need to pay the current $5.

### Participating preferred

Receives its fixed dividend **plus** a possible additional dividend if the company does especially well (usually tied to the common dividend exceeding some level). Investor-friendly. Rare in practice but tested.

### Convertible preferred

Can be exchanged for a fixed number of common shares at the holder's option. The **conversion ratio** = par ÷ conversion price. A $100 par preferred convertible at $25 converts into 4 common shares.

- **Parity price** of the common = preferred market price ÷ conversion ratio. If the preferred trades at $120 and converts into 4 shares, parity for the common is $30. If the common trades above $30, converting is profitable.
- Convertible preferred prices **track the common stock** when the common is near or above parity — so it has growth potential, unlike other preferred.
- Investor-friendly → lower dividend. Conversion is not a taxable event.

### Callable preferred

The issuer may buy it back at a stated **call price** (par or a small premium) after a stated date. Issuers call preferred when interest rates **fall** so they can reissue at a lower dividend. Issuer-friendly → investors demand a **higher** dividend. Call protection (a period during which it can't be called) and the call premium partly compensate investors. Callable preferred faces **call risk / reinvestment risk** for the holder.

### Adjustable-rate (floating-rate / variable-rate) preferred

The dividend is reset periodically (e.g., quarterly) based on a benchmark such as Treasury bill rates. Because the dividend floats with market rates, the **price stays close to par** — this type has the **least interest-rate risk** among preferred stocks.

### Summary table

| Feature | Benefits | Effect on dividend rate | Key point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Straight | — | Highest of basics | Missed dividends lost |
| Cumulative | Investor | Lower | Arrears must be paid before common |
| Participating | Investor | Lower | Fixed + possible extra |
| Convertible | Investor | Lower | Tracks common; conversion ratio = par ÷ conversion price |
| Callable | Issuer | Higher | Called when rates fall |
| Adjustable-rate | Investor (price stability) | Varies with rates | Price stable near par |

## 2.3 Suitability

**Best for:**

- Investors seeking **steady income** higher than common dividends, who accept equity-level (not bond-level) safety.
- **Corporations** investing excess cash — the dividends-received deduction makes preferred very tax-efficient for corporate holders.
- Investors who want income plus some growth → **convertible preferred**.
- Investors who want income with less interest-rate risk → **adjustable-rate preferred**.

**Not appropriate for:**

- Investors seeking **capital appreciation** (except convertible).
- Investors who cannot tolerate price declines when interest rates rise.
- Investors who need a guaranteed payment — a bond is safer because interest is a legal obligation; preferred dividends are not.

**Preferred vs. common vs. bonds — the comparison the exam wants:**

| | Common stock | Preferred stock | Corporate bond |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ownership or debt | Equity | Equity | Debt |
| Payment | Variable dividend (not required) | Fixed dividend (not required but prioritized) | Fixed interest (legally required) |
| Voting | Yes | Usually no | No |
| Maturity | None | None | Yes |
| Liquidation priority | Last | After creditors, before common | Before all stockholders |
| Growth potential | High | Low (unless convertible) | None (unless convertible) |
| Interest-rate risk | Low/indirect | High | High |

## Chapter 2 quick review

- Fixed dividend based on **par ($100 typical)**; priority over common for dividends and in liquidation; usually no vote, no maturity.
- Price moves **inversely to interest rates** (like a bond). Adjustable-rate preferred is the exception — price stays near par.
- **Cumulative**: arrears + current must be paid before any common dividend.
- **Convertible**: conversion ratio = par ÷ conversion price; tracks the common; growth potential.
- **Callable**: issuer calls when rates fall; investors get a higher dividend to compensate.
- **Participating**: fixed dividend plus possible bonus.
- Corporations love preferred for the **dividends-received deduction**.
