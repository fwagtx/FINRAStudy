# Chapter 7: Investment Companies

> Mutual funds, closed-end funds, ETFs and UITs. This is one of the most heavily tested product areas. Know the Investment Company Act of 1940 classifications, how NAV and POP work, sales charge rules and breakpoints, share classes, Subchapter M, and the ETF/closed-end/open-end comparisons.

---

## 7.1 Foundations

An **investment company** pools money from many investors and invests it according to a stated objective, giving each investor an undivided interest in a diversified, professionally managed portfolio. Governed by the **Investment Company Act of 1940**.

**Advantages for investors:** diversification, professional management, liquidity, low minimums, convenience (automatic reinvestment, exchanges within a fund family), record-keeping. **Disadvantages:** fees and expenses, no control over individual holdings, tax inefficiency (capital gains distributions), and for mutual funds, pricing only once per day.

**Three classifications under the 1940 Act:**

1. **Face-amount certificate companies** — issue certificates promising a fixed sum at a future date in exchange for lump-sum or periodic payments. Nearly extinct.
2. **Unit investment trusts (UITs)** — fixed, unmanaged portfolios; redeemable units; no board of directors (see 7.6).
3. **Management companies** — actively or passively managed portfolios with a board of directors. Subdivided into:
   - **Open-end** (mutual funds) — continuously issue and redeem shares; no secondary market (7.3).
   - **Closed-end** — fixed number of shares sold in an IPO, then trade on exchanges (7.4).

Management companies are further labeled **diversified** or **non-diversified**. A **diversified** company meets the **75-5-10 test**: at least 75% of its assets are invested such that no more than **5%** of total assets is in any one issuer and the fund owns no more than **10%** of any one issuer's voting shares (the other 25% is unrestricted).

**Registration.** An investment company must register with the SEC under the 1940 Act and its shares are registered under the Securities Act of 1933 (prospectus required). To make a public offering it needs at least **$100,000** in net worth (seed capital).

**Key people.**

| Role | Function |
|---|---|
| **Board of directors** | Oversees the fund; hires the adviser, custodian, transfer agent; at least **40% must be independent** (non-interested) directors |
| **Investment adviser (portfolio manager)** | Manages the portfolio per the objective; paid a management fee (the largest expense); contract approved by the board and shareholders; initially 2 years, renewed annually |
| **Custodian** (usually a bank) | Holds the fund's cash and securities; safekeeping |
| **Transfer agent** | Issues/redeems shares, keeps shareholder records, sends statements and distributions |
| **Underwriter/distributor (sponsor)** | Markets the shares to broker-dealers and the public; collects the sales charge |

**Shareholder voting rights.** Shareholders elect the board, approve the adviser's contract, and must approve any change in the fund's **investment objective**, changes in fees/12b-1 plans, and changes from diversified to non-diversified. A mutual fund **cannot** change its objective without a majority vote of shareholders.

## 7.2 Types of funds

Funds are classified by **investment objective**, which is stated in the prospectus and determines suitability.

| Fund type | Objective / holdings | Suitable for |
|---|---|---|
| **Money market fund** | Capital preservation & liquidity; short-term debt (CP, T-bills, repos); maintains stable **$1.00 NAV** (not guaranteed); no sales charge; check-writing | Emergency funds, parking cash |
| **Growth fund** | Capital appreciation; stocks of growing companies; little income | Long-horizon investors |
| **Aggressive growth / small-cap** | Maximum appreciation; volatile | High risk tolerance |
| **Value fund** | Undervalued stocks | Moderate |
| **Income (equity income) fund** | Dividend income; utilities, blue chips, preferred | Income seekers |
| **Growth & income** | Mix of both | Balanced needs |
| **Balanced fund** | Stocks **and** bonds, roughly fixed proportions (e.g., 60/40) | Moderate, one-fund solution |
| **Asset allocation fund** | Stocks, bonds and cash, proportions adjusted by the manager | Moderate |
| **Target-date (lifecycle) fund** | Allocation becomes more conservative as the target date approaches ("glide path") | Retirement savers; 401(k) default options |
| **Bond (fixed-income) fund** | Income; corporate, government, muni or high-yield bonds | Income; interest-rate risk |
| **Municipal bond fund** | Federally tax-free income | High-bracket taxable accounts; **never in an IRA** |
| **Index fund** | Replicate an index (S&P 500); passive; **lowest expenses**, low turnover, tax efficient | Cost-conscious investors |
| **Sector / specialized fund** | One industry (tech, health care, energy, gold) — concentrated, more risk | Investors wanting targeted exposure |
| **International fund** | Non-US companies; currency risk | Diversification |
| **Global fund** | US **and** non-US | Diversification |
| **Emerging markets** | Developing countries; high risk | Aggressive |
| **Fund of funds** | Holds other mutual funds; layered fees | Simplicity |
| **Hedge-like / alternative strategy funds** | Long/short, leverage, derivatives inside a '40 Act wrapper | Sophisticated |

**Risk spectrum (lowest → highest):** money market → government bond → investment-grade bond → balanced → growth & income → growth → aggressive growth / sector / emerging markets.

## 7.3 Open-end management companies (mutual funds)

### 7.3.1 Characteristics

- **Continuous offering** — the fund issues new shares whenever investors buy; it is always a primary offering, so a **prospectus** must be delivered with or before every solicitation.
- **Redeemable** — investors sell shares back **to the fund** (not to other investors) at the next-computed **NAV**. The fund must pay redemption proceeds within **7 calendar days**.
- **No secondary market.** Shares aren't traded on exchanges.
- Only **common stock** is issued — no preferred, no bonds. Mutual funds may not issue senior securities; they generally cannot buy on margin or sell short (and are limited in borrowing).
- **Fractional shares** are allowed.
- Priced once per day using **forward pricing** — orders are filled at the next NAV calculated after the order is received (NAV is computed at the close, 4:00 pm ET).
- Shares can be purchased from the fund, through its underwriter, or through broker-dealers that have a selling agreement with the underwriter. A broker-dealer may **not** hold itself out as a buyer of fund shares at a discount or maintain an inventory.

### 7.3.2 Shareholder rights

- Vote for the board, on the adviser contract, on changes to the investment objective and fundamental policies, and on fee changes.
- Receive **semiannual reports** (annual report must be audited) with financial statements and the portfolio holdings.
- Receive dividends and capital gains distributions pro-rata.
- Right to redeem at NAV at any time the market is open.
- Right to a prospectus and, on request, the **Statement of Additional Information (SAI)**.

**The prospectus** (summary prospectus permitted) contains: investment objective and strategies, risks, **fee table** (sales charges and annual operating expenses), performance (1-, 5-, 10-year returns), management, purchase and redemption procedures, and tax information. Performance shown must be as of the most recent calendar quarter and must be accompanied by the "past performance does not guarantee future results" disclosure.

### 7.3.3 Transactions

**Net asset value (NAV) per share.**

```
NAV per share = (Total assets − Total liabilities) ÷ Shares outstanding
```

NAV changes when the portfolio's securities change in value, when dividends/interest are received, or when distributions are paid out. NAV does **not** change when investors buy or redeem shares (money and shares move proportionally).

**Public offering price (POP)** for a fund with a front-end load:

```
POP = NAV ÷ (1 − Sales charge %)
Sales charge % = (POP − NAV) ÷ POP
```

The sales charge is always calculated as a percentage of the **POP** (offering price), not the NAV.

**Example.** NAV = $19.00, sales charge 5%. POP = 19 / 0.95 = **$20.00**. The $1.00 goes to the underwriter/selling dealer. If quoted "NAV 19.00, POP 20.00," sales charge = 1/20 = 5%.

**Maximum sales charge.** FINRA Rule 2341 caps the sales charge on mutual fund shares at **8.5%** of the POP — and a fund may only charge the maximum if it offers **all** of: breakpoints, rights of accumulation, and automatic dividend reinvestment at NAV. Funds that also charge a 12b-1 fee have a lower cap (6.25%–7.25%); the exam tests the 8.5% figure.

**No-load fund** — sold at NAV with no sales charge (may still have a 12b-1 fee of up to **0.25%** and still call itself no-load).

**Breakpoints** — reduced sales charges for larger purchases. A typical schedule: under $25,000 → 5.75%; $25k–$50k → 5.0%; $50k–$100k → 4.5%; $100k+ → 3.5%; $1 million+ → 0%. Breakpoints are available to individuals, married couples, parents with minor children (custodial accounts), and most trusts/corporations — **not** to investment clubs or groups formed to pool purchases.

Ways to qualify for a breakpoint:

- **Letter of intent (LOI)** — investor states an intent to invest enough to hit a breakpoint within **13 months**; receives the reduced charge immediately on all purchases. Can be **backdated up to 90 days** to include a recent purchase. If the investor doesn't complete it, the fund retroactively collects the higher charge (shares are held in escrow). Appreciation does not count toward the LOI; only new money does.
- **Rights of accumulation (ROA)** — once the **current value** of all holdings in the fund family (plus the new purchase) crosses a breakpoint, the lower charge applies to **future** purchases (not retroactive). No time limit; appreciation counts.
- **Combination privilege** — combining purchases across funds within the same family to reach a breakpoint.

**Breakpoint sale** — a **prohibited** practice: a rep sells an amount just below a breakpoint (e.g., $24,000 when the breakpoint is $25,000) to earn a higher commission, or splits a purchase across fund families so no breakpoint is reached. Reps must inform customers of breakpoints.

**Other privileges.** **Exchange (conversion) privilege** — switch between funds in the same family at NAV with no new sales charge (still a taxable event). **Automatic reinvestment** of distributions at NAV. **Systematic withdrawal plans**. **Dollar cost averaging** — investing a fixed dollar amount at regular intervals, buying more shares when prices are low and fewer when high; results in an average cost per share **lower** than the average price per share over time (doesn't guarantee profit).

**Redemption.** At the next NAV, less any **redemption fee** or **contingent deferred sales charge (CDSC)**. Must be paid within **7 days**. Some funds impose short-term redemption fees to discourage market timing. Redemption is a **taxable event** (capital gain/loss).

### 7.3.4 Returns

A mutual fund investor earns a return from three sources:

1. **Dividend distributions** — from the net investment income (dividends and interest the fund receives, minus expenses). Taxed to the shareholder as ordinary or qualified dividends.
2. **Capital gains distributions** — from gains the fund realized selling portfolio securities, usually paid once a year. **Long-term** capital gains distributions are taxed as long-term gains to the shareholder **regardless of how long the shareholder has owned the fund**. Short-term gains are passed through as ordinary dividends.
3. **Unrealized appreciation** — the increase in NAV, realized only when the investor sells.

**Total return** = (ending NAV − beginning NAV + distributions) ÷ beginning NAV.

Distributions are taxable whether taken in cash or **reinvested**. Reinvested distributions **increase the cost basis**. A fund's NAV drops by the amount of a distribution on the ex-date; buying right before a big distribution ("buying a dividend") gives the investor an immediate tax bill with no economic gain — a sales practice reps must not encourage.

**Expense ratio** = annual operating expenses (management fee + 12b-1 fee + other) ÷ average net assets. Lower is better; index funds often under 0.10%, actively managed equity funds commonly 0.5%–1.5%.

**12b-1 fees** — annual distribution/marketing fees charged against fund assets, named after SEC Rule 12b-1. Must be approved by the board (including a majority of independent directors) and shareholders, and reviewed annually. Max **0.75%** for distribution plus **0.25%** for service fees (total 1.00%). A fund calling itself "no-load" may charge at most 0.25%.

### 7.3.5 Share classes

The same portfolio sold with different fee structures. The exam tests which class fits which investor.

| Class | Sales charge | 12b-1 fee | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Class A** | **Front-end load** (deducted from the purchase); breakpoints available | Low (≈0.25%) | **Large investments** (breakpoints) and **long holding periods** — lowest total cost over time |
| **Class B** | **Back-end load (CDSC)** that declines each year, usually to 0% after 6–8 years, then shares **convert to Class A** | Higher (≈1.00%) | Smaller amounts held long-term; **never for large investments** (no breakpoints — a $1 million Class B purchase is a red flag) |
| **Class C** | Level load — small or no front load, 1% CDSC if sold within the first year | Higher (≈1.00%), **ongoing, never converts** | **Short-term** holding periods (1–3 years); most expensive if held long-term |
| **Class I / institutional** | None | None/low | Institutions, retirement plans, advisory accounts |
| **Class R** | None | Varies | Retirement plans |

A CDSC is assessed on the **lesser** of the original purchase price or the current value at redemption.

**Rule of thumb:** Big money or long horizon → A. Small money, long horizon → B (or A). Short horizon → C.

### 7.3.6 Subchapter M

Under **Subchapter M of the Internal Revenue Code**, a fund that qualifies as a **regulated investment company (RIC)** acts as a **conduit (pipeline)**: it pays no corporate tax on income it distributes to shareholders. To qualify, the fund must distribute at least **90%** of its **net investment income** to shareholders. (It then pays tax only on whatever it retains.) Shareholders pay tax on the distributions. Nearly all mutual funds, closed-end funds, ETFs, REITs (which have a similar 90% rule) qualify.

If a fund distributes 90%+ of its income, it is taxed only on the undistributed portion; if it fails the 90% test, it is taxed on **all** its income like a regular corporation.

## 7.4 Closed-end management companies

A **closed-end fund (CEF)** raises capital **once** in an IPO (with a prospectus), issuing a fixed number of shares. After that:

- Shares trade in the **secondary market** (NYSE, Nasdaq, OTC) between investors; the fund does **not** redeem them. Bought and sold through a broker like a stock, paying **commissions** (not sales charges).
- Price is set by **supply and demand**, so shares can trade at a **premium** or (more often) a **discount** to NAV. The NAV is still calculated and published, but the market price can be anything.
- Can issue **preferred stock and bonds** (leverage) in addition to common — unlike open-end funds.
- Can be bought on margin and sold short; can be traded intraday.
- Often used for less-liquid assets (muni bonds, emerging market debt) because the manager never has to sell holdings to meet redemptions.
- **Interval funds** are a hybrid that offers periodic repurchases at NAV.

| Feature | Open-end (mutual fund) | Closed-end fund |
|---|---|---|
| Capitalization | Continuous; unlimited shares | One-time IPO; fixed shares |
| Where bought/sold | From/to the fund (via underwriter/dealers) | Secondary market (exchange) |
| Price | NAV + sales charge (forward pricing) | Supply & demand; premium/discount to NAV |
| Cost to trade | Sales charge (load) or no-load | Brokerage commission |
| Securities issued | Common only | Common, preferred, bonds |
| Fractional shares | Yes | No |
| Margin / short | No | Yes |
| Prospectus | Always (continuous offering) | Only at IPO |

## 7.5 Exchange-traded funds

### 7.5.1 Passive ETFs

An **exchange-traded fund** holds a basket of securities and trades on an exchange **throughout the day** like a stock. Most ETFs are **passive** — they track an index (S&P 500, total bond market, gold price, a sector). Legally most are registered as open-end companies or UITs under the 1940 Act, but they behave very differently from mutual funds:

- **Intraday trading** at market prices; can be bought on **margin**, **sold short**, and traded with limit/stop orders.
- Price stays very close to NAV because of the **creation/redemption** mechanism: large institutions ("authorized participants") exchange baskets of the underlying securities for large blocks of ETF shares (creation units) and vice versa, arbitraging away any premium or discount.
- **Low expense ratios** (passive) and **tax-efficient** (in-kind creation/redemption means few capital gains distributions).
- Investors pay **brokerage commissions** (though many brokers now charge zero) and the **bid-ask spread**, not sales charges. Frequent small purchases can rack up costs — a mutual fund may be better for dollar-cost averaging.
- Dividends are paid to shareholders; distributions taxed like a mutual fund's.
- Buy and sell through a broker; a prospectus is available but not necessarily delivered on each secondary purchase.

| | Mutual fund | ETF |
|---|---|---|
| Pricing | Once daily at NAV (forward pricing) | Continuous, market price ≈ NAV |
| Trading | With the fund | On the exchange |
| Margin / short / limit orders | No | Yes |
| Typical management | Active or passive | Mostly passive (active ETFs exist) |
| Costs | Sales charge, expense ratio | Commission, spread, (low) expense ratio |
| Tax efficiency | Lower | Higher |

### 7.5.2 Other ETFs

- **Actively managed ETFs** — a manager picks securities; higher expenses.
- **Leveraged ETFs** — use derivatives to deliver **2× or 3×** the **daily** return of an index. Because the leverage resets daily, over periods longer than a day the return **compounds and diverges** from the index multiple — especially in volatile markets. Not suitable for buy-and-hold investors; FINRA considers them complex products requiring heightened suitability review.
- **Inverse ETFs** — deliver the **opposite** of the index's daily return (−1×, −2×, −3×), letting investors bet against a market without shorting. Same daily-reset problem.
- **Commodity ETFs** — hold physical gold/silver or commodity futures. Futures-based ones suffer from contango/roll costs and may be taxed as partnerships (K-1s).
- **Currency, volatility (VIX), and thematic ETFs.**
- **Smart beta / factor ETFs** — rule-based tilts (value, momentum, low volatility).

### 7.5.3 Exchange-traded notes

An **ETN** is an **unsecured debt obligation of a bank** (not a fund) that promises to pay the return of an index at maturity, minus fees. The investor owns **no portfolio** — just the bank's promise. Therefore ETNs carry the **credit risk of the issuing bank** in addition to market risk (Lehman Brothers ETNs became worthless in 2008). They trade on exchanges like ETFs, can track hard-to-hold assets (commodities, volatility), and have no tracking error. Interest/dividends are not paid; return comes at sale or maturity (usually taxed as capital gain). Complex product — suitability scrutiny.

## 7.6 Unit investment trusts

A **UIT** is an investment company that holds a **fixed, unmanaged portfolio** of securities (bonds or stocks) for a set period and then terminates, distributing the proceeds.

- **No board of directors, no investment adviser, no active management** — the portfolio is assembled once by the sponsor and then "supervised" (securities are only sold if credit deteriorates).
- Organized under a **trust indenture** with a trustee.
- Investors buy **units** (redeemable at NAV from the sponsor, which often maintains a secondary market). Sold with a sales charge via a prospectus.
- **Fixed termination date.** Bond UITs pay monthly income and terminate when the bonds mature; equity UITs typically run 1–5 years.
- Low ongoing expenses (no manager), but a sales charge up front and little flexibility.
- Many **variable annuity separate accounts** and some ETFs (e.g., the original SPDR S&P 500) are legally UITs.

**Suitability:** investors who want a known, diversified, buy-and-hold portfolio with predictable income (bond UITs) and no manager risk.

## 7.7 Alpha and beta

Portfolio performance measures that show up in fund fact sheets and on the exam.

- **Beta** — a measure of a security's or fund's **systematic (market) risk**: how much it moves relative to the overall market (beta of the market = **1.0**).
  - Beta 1.5 → expected to move 1.5× the market (more volatile, aggressive).
  - Beta 0.5 → moves half as much (defensive, less volatile).
  - Beta 0 → uncorrelated with the market (e.g., cash); negative beta moves opposite (rare — gold at times).
  - Beta measures **non-diversifiable** risk; a diversified portfolio's risk is essentially its beta.
- **Alpha** — the return a fund earns **above (or below) what its beta would predict**; the portion attributable to the manager's skill (or luck). Alpha of +2 means the manager beat the risk-adjusted expectation by 2 percentage points. Positive alpha is the goal of active management; index funds target alpha of zero.
- **Standard deviation** — a measure of **total volatility** (both systematic and unsystematic). Higher standard deviation = wider swings = more risk.
- **Sharpe ratio** — (return − risk-free rate) ÷ standard deviation; return per unit of total risk. Higher is better.
- **R-squared** — how much of a fund's movement is explained by its benchmark (100 = perfect index tracker).
- **Correlation** — ranges from +1 (move together) to −1 (move opposite). Diversification works best with assets that have low or negative correlation.

**Modern portfolio theory (MPT)** — combine assets with low correlation to reduce risk for a given expected return; the set of optimal combinations is the **efficient frontier**. **Capital asset pricing model (CAPM)**: expected return = risk-free rate + beta × (market return − risk-free rate).

**Example.** Risk-free rate 3%, market return 9%, fund beta 1.2. Expected return = 3% + 1.2 × (9% − 3%) = 10.2%. If the fund actually returned 12%, its alpha is **+1.8%**.

## Chapter 7 quick review

- 1940 Act: face-amount certificate cos., UITs, management companies (open-end vs. closed-end). Diversified = **75-5-10**. $100,000 net worth to go public. Board ≥ 40% independent. Objective change needs shareholder vote.
- Mutual fund: continuous primary offering (prospectus always), redeemable at NAV (paid within **7 days**), forward pricing, common stock only, no margin/short.
- **NAV = (assets − liabilities) ÷ shares. POP = NAV ÷ (1 − SC%).** Sales charge is a % of POP. Max **8.5%** (only with breakpoints, ROA, reinvestment at NAV). No-load → 12b-1 ≤ 0.25%; 12b-1 max 1.00% total.
- Breakpoints: LOI (13 months, backdate 90 days, new money only), ROA (current value, future purchases), combination privilege. **Breakpoint sales are prohibited.** No breakpoints for investment clubs.
- Share classes: **A** = front-end + breakpoints (big/long); **B** = CDSC, converts to A (small/long); **C** = level load, never converts (short-term).
- Subchapter M: distribute ≥ **90%** of net investment income → fund untaxed on distributed amounts (conduit).
- Distributions taxable whether reinvested or not; LT cap gains distributions are LT regardless of holding period; reinvestment raises basis.
- Closed-end: IPO once, trades on exchange at premium/discount to NAV, commissions, can issue preferred/debt, margin OK.
- ETFs: intraday, margin/short OK, low cost, tax efficient; leveraged/inverse reset **daily** — not buy-and-hold. ETNs = bank debt → credit risk, no portfolio.
- UIT: fixed, unmanaged, no board/adviser, terminates, units redeemable.
- Beta = market risk vs. market (1.0); alpha = excess return from manager skill; standard deviation = total risk; Sharpe = return per unit of risk.
